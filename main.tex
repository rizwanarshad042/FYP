\section*{Abstract}
\thispagestyle{empty}

Such retailers with products available in multiple sizes, variants, or branches have instances where they end up running out of supply of popular products in some places and accumulating stocks of those same products in other places. Large retailers overcome this problem through enterprise forecasting systems such as SAP and Blue Yonder. However, this solution is not affordable to small business owners. This research creates StockSense which is a multi-tenant SaaS system that has a generic forecasting engine for all companies that have inventories. A free point of sale (POS) application will be used to track the sales and inventory, whereas paid applications will be used to forecast demands per variant and location, suggest redistributions, early markdowns and reorder recommendations each with an explanation and cost savings associated. Redistribution takes priority with alerts being issued in advance to prevent the items from becoming dead stock. The businesses using other POS or spreadsheets will join the system by uploading their historical sales reports.

\section*{Executive Summary}
\thispagestyle{empty}

There are two opposing problems that plague retailers on a daily basis. Dead stock is taking space in the stores for several months without moving and tying down capital, while stockouts are losing customers since there is no stock of the desired product. This problem is faced by any retailer who manages inventory across variants or sizes or even across the stores. It is more of an issue for small businesses where restocking decisions are made based on instinct rather than data. Large businesses use enterprise demand forecasting solutions like SAP and Blue Yonder. But these systems are costly and require a data team and an ERP system. Small businesses that have just POS or use Excel sheets to keep track of their inventory have nothing comparable, and the lightweight tools that they have forecast per product rather than per size, only alert them once the stock is low and treat each store individually. StockSense is a multi-tenant SaaS platform built to solve this problem. A free POS solution helps to record sales and track stock to bring all data into the platform. A paid subscription includes forecasts at the variant and location level, markdown optimization, and stock redistribution recommendations. Those who have been using some other tool, they can integrate by providing past sales in CSV or Excel format.

StockSense stands out among existing solutions in three ways. First, it prioritizes redistribution since it is generally cheaper to move unused stock to a branch where it sells rather than discount it or order more. Second, it is proactive since it identifies items as soon as their sales begin to drop instead of waiting until they have become dead stock. Third, it explains each recommendation in clear terms and provides an approximate financial impact to ensure the recommendations are trusted by the owner. The background research included a survey of forecasting and inventory literature, a survey of existing enterprise and small-business platforms, as well as the selection of public datasets from retailers to use in training the initial models. There exists no existing platform offering a combination of a free POS and a paid service for reducing dead stock in small retailers. The accuracy of forecasts will be evaluated based on standard error metrics, while the reduction in dead stock will be estimated through a replay of past sales.

\chapter{Introduction}

For retailers, having the right product at the right place, at the right time, and having the right amount to sell is a critical need and is often the responsibility of spreadsheets, stripped down POS systems, or manual records, with very little analysis of sales data to do. This results in stockouts, stock deadlocks particularly where the same product has varying demand in size and between branches. This involves enterprise forecasting and inventory optimization technologies that are used by larger retailers, but which are rather costly and challenging to manage without experts. Because of this, many small businesses make decisions on what to order and where to place it based on gut feelings, even though data to make a more informed decision is already in their own sales records.

StockSense is suggested as a multi-tenant, SaaS-based solution that will help smaller businesses gain access to data-driven inventory management. It includes a free POS system, demand forecasting, stock redistribution between branches, and markdown optimization and recommendations, with explanations of why. It utilizes data that retailers already gather with their sales, leaving the retailer with the ability to see the risk in inventory early and take action in time before it becomes unsold stock or out of stock. It does not require any technical training capability on the part of the owner because all forecasts and recommendations are in plain business language. This chapter states the purpose of the document, its intended audience and the definitions, acronyms and abbreviations used throughout the report.

\chapter{Project Vision}

The idea behind StockSense is to provide a cheap solution to small to medium sized stores to better understand their sales and also use data to inform their stock decisions. It is applicable to any retailer selling physical products that come in multiple variants (or sizes) or in more than one store, such as clothing, footwear, etc., where demand fluctuates significantly across products, sizes and stores. In these enterprises one product may be out in one size at one branch while the same product may not even sell at another branch or in another size. The vision is that it will be a platform that a small business can start using as their daily sales system without any cost and once they gather sufficient sales data it will provide useful feedback on what to reorder, what to shift between store locations, what to cut in price sooner. This chapter introduces the problem domain, problem statement, goals and scope of the project, the related sustainable development goals, the constraints, the business opportunity and the stakeholders of the system.

\section{Problem Domain Overview}

RM is the activity of making decisions regarding the selection of products to be sold, the amount of those products to be sold, and the place where the products are sold. Small businesses make these choices typically by instinct rather than by formal forecasting, and only when there's an empty shelf or excess unsold inventory becomes apparent. StockSense does this by pulling in past sales information and applying this to predict the future demand for each product, size and location, and then applying this to make recommendations to the user, including making decisions to reorder, move stock from one branch to another or sell stock that is nearing expiry. The system is a multi-tenant SaaS system, where each business is an independent tenant, has their own products, stores, inventory, sales, forecasts and recommendations, and their data is inaccessible to other tenants. Existing businesses that already have an external point of sale system or who have a spreadsheet-based system can upload their historic sales reports as CSV or Excel files, and any other businesses can capture their sales right from the built-in point of sale system. For both, the forecasting and recommendation features use the same data.

\section{Problem Statement}

Converting past sales data into inventory decisions at the product, size and location level is challenging for retailers. This information would otherwise lead owners to order the same volume for every branch and all sizes, leading to overstocking, stock-outs, slow inventory and the loss of revenue from selling popular items that don't sell because they're out of stock. Current enterprise solutions that address the problem are expensive, and inexpensive tools for small businesses merely track inventory and alert the salesman when it's low. It is therefore necessary to have an integrated and cost-effective solution that captures real-time sales, predicts detailed demand and suggests specific inventory actions with a clear explanation of each action, enabling small retailers to manage their inventory driven based on data, not intuition. One of the challenges is that small retailers don't have the time or expertise to develop such analysis for themselves, even for businesses that maintain good records, they don't use such analysis to plan stock.

\section{Problem Elaboration}

The above problem has multiple components, each of which defines a requirement for the system. Many retailers do not have a tool to analyse their past sales and the tools that can are intended for organizations with a dedicated planning team. However, even if a forecast is available, it is frequently product level only which mask the vast variations in demand from store to store and between sizes of the same product. As each branch operates independently, one store could have surplus stock of a particular product that another branch is in desperate need of. Slow moving items are typically discovered when they're already dead stock and the only way to get rid of them is by discounting. Last, owners will be less inclined to act on recommendations due to a “black box” reasoning behind them, created by a machine learning (ML) model. The following is a summary of the main issues addressed in StockSense.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Retailers lack the tools to analyze their previous sales data.
    \item Existing enterprise demand forecasting solutions are expensive and technically complex for small businesses.
    \item Demand differs between stores, so aggregate product-level forecasts are not enough.
    \item Demand also differs significantly between product sizes and variants.
    \item One store may have excess inventory while another branch needs the same product.
    \item Slow-moving inventory is often identified only after it has already become dead stock.
    \item Business owners find ML-based recommendations difficult to trust when the reasoning is not explained.
\end{itemize}

\section{Goals and Objectives}

The long-term vision of StockSense is to enable small retailers to have the same inventory planning capabilities as large retailers, at a level of cost and complexity they can afford. To get there, the project needs to provide a secure multi-tenant platform, a reliable method for feeding this data into the platform, a forecasting model that doesn't require a lot of history to work and a platform that can recommend reducing dead stock instead of taking new orders. All recommendations should also be presented with an explanation to build trusting relationships. This also includes the business model as part of the goals, since the sales data stream is what pulls it onto the platform in the first place is the free POS layer. Specific project goals are provided below. The aims are also selected so that each can be assessed at the conclusion of the project either by a working feature or a measured result.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Design a multi-tenant platform that allows multiple small businesses to securely manage their sales and inventory data.
    \item Develop a data ingestion and cleaning pipeline for CSV and Excel sales reports that normalizes the data into a schema of product, size, store, date and quantity sold.
    \item Develop a demand forecasting model for product-size-location combinations.
    \item Develop a recommendation engine that determines what to reorder, how much to reorder and where existing inventory should be redistributed.
    \item Develop a markdown optimizer for slow-moving stock.
    \item Provide an explanation and an estimated financial impact for every recommendation.
    \item Evaluate forecast accuracy with standard error metrics and estimate the potential reduction in dead stock through historical simulation.
    \item Offer the platform under a freemium model with a free POS layer and paid forecasting and recommendation features.
\end{itemize}

\section{Project Scope}

StockSense is designed for companies who sell a variant and size product, such as clothing, footwear or any other physical product, in multiple stores, experiencing stockouts and dead stock. The platform can be used by several businesses, each of which can be a separate tenant, with stores, products, inventory and sales history being maintained separately for each tenant. In this context, the system will offer multi-customer business management, store, product and size management, sales and inventory management via the point of sale, importing of historical data from CSV and excel files, data cleaning and normalisation, forecasting demands at product, size and location level, recommendations for redistribution and reorder, recommendations for markdowns, explanations with an estimated financial impact for each recommendation and dashboards visualisation. Forecasting will be done on weekly, monthly, rather than longer time scales; this corresponds with the time that small retailers consider for reordering and transfer decisions. Initially, this is not a fully automatic integration with external POS systems, but manual and batch upload of data from external systems using CSV and Excel files will be the primary method of integration.

\subsection{SDG-8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth)}

Small businesses are among the primary job providers but struggle to find the analytics tools that are essential to large retailers' success and are too expensive for them. At this time, demand forecasting and inventory optimization have largely been available to enterprise systems and data teams of large chains. These features are accessible to small grocery stores with StockSense's free Point of Sale (POS) and affordable subscription, enabling them to sell more of what's popular with their customers, lower loss rates on unsold merchandise, and invest their capital in a more effective way. A store with lower stockout rate and less of a discount/loss of inventory is in a better position to retain their employees, open new store locations and to hire more employees. The project contributes to productive employment and sustained economic growth in the small business sector – the focus of SDG-8 – in this way.

\subsection{SDG-9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure)}

StockSense is bringing data-driven innovation to an industry where it hasn't been seen much—retail. It transforms the usual sales data into forecasts and recommendations, thus allowing retailers to make better use of their capital, store space and stock. When goods are sold out to other branches, and slow movers are discounted before they become unsellable, it will result in lesser accumulation of unsold goods that are either donated or sold at a loss. \section{Constraints}

StockSense comes with several restrictions when designing and testing. The first is the shortage and poor quality of past sales data, as small retailers tend to have incomplete and inconsistent data. In addition, a new business will need to build or import sufficient volume of sales information before accurate forecasts are possible, and the accuracy of the forecasts will always be dependent on the quality and quantity of information that is available to them. Results obtained on public retail datasets may not accurately reflect the behavior of small retailers, and should be interpreted with caution. The system is based first of all on physical products, such as clothing and footwear, and on short- to medium-term forecasts rather than long-term planning. Last but not least, redistribution is not always cost effective - transporting and handling stock between branches and transfer delays will add costs, and the risk of stock out at the branch that the stock is being transferred to could result in lost sales. The recommendation engine then needs to calculate these costs and the predicted benefit before recommending a transfer.

\section{Business Opportunity}

StockSense has a freemium SaaS business model. The basic POS functions are complimentary, which indicates that small enterprises will be able to keep track of inventory and sales without having to pay a subscription fee, eliminating one of the primary obstacles facing small shops when choosing new software. Advanced features like demand forecasting and inventory redistribution, markdown optimization, benchmarking and recommendations with estimated financial impact come with a paid subscription. The free tier already contains all the data of the business's sales history, so there's no additional setup required when upgrading to the paid tier, and the value of the added paid features can be illustrated using the business's own data. This model will enable companies to begin using the platform with minimal up-front investment and to provide the expanding stores with a seamless journey towards advanced analytics. It also provides StockSense with a massive user base from which paying customers can be recruited and the subscription fees pay the costs of operating the forecasting infrastructure provided to all tenants.

\section{Detailed Literature Review}

The papers presented in this section have been chosen because they relate to a particular aspect of StockSense design. The literature on fashion retail indicates that forecasting demand for new products is particularly challenging because they typically lack sales history and that product characteristics can play a role in compensating for the lack of sales history~\cite{singh2019fashion}. The use of demand forecasting with price optimization has also been demonstrated to boost revenues in online retail, lending credence to the concept of tying forecasting to price decisions, such as markdowns~\cite{ferreira2016analytics}. Fifteen papers are discussed in detail in the following subsections. With each paper, a summary of the paper is followed by a critical analysis and an explanation of the connection between the paper and StockSense, both in the same format as the project template. The fifteen papers collectively deal with forecasting techniques, decisions on inventory, pricing, the connection between forecasting and action, and the confidence users have in the forecast's accuracy.

\subsection{Deep Neural Networks for Fashion Sales Forecasting}

Loureiro, Migu\'eis and da Silva's 2018 article ``Exploring the use of deep neural networks for sales forecasting in fashion retail'' is reviewed here. The article was selected because it compares deep learning to more basic models for predicting the sale of fashion items, which are akin to the items StockSense aims to predict. It is summarized and critically analysed in the next subsections and its relation to StockSense is provided.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Loureiro, Migu\'eis and da Silva~\cite{loureiro2018deep} compare the performance of their deep neural network to that of shallow networks like decision trees, random forests, support vector regression and standard neural networks for predicting the sales of new products in the fashion industry. The study is based on a real dataset from a fashion retailer, where each product is represented with attributes pertaining to its physical properties and its distribution, and predictive variables representing the domain knowledge of the retailer's experts. Since this new item doesn't have a sales history itself, the models learn how these factors are related to the sales history of past products. The authors report that the deep learning model performs well in forecasting and is competitive compared with the other models; some simpler ensemble models such as random forests are also good forecasting models. The paper argues that data-driven models can be used to better plan new collections than with only expert judgment.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

The paper's biggest contribution is the use of real company data and not a synthetic data set to evaluate the models, which makes the results applicable in practice. It also demonstrates that combining product attributes with expert knowledge is a useful approach for predicting product demand when there is no historical data. But this study is on the total sales of new products for an entire season, and is not a prediction of store sales or size sales. The data is from one large company with an existing data infrastructure and many products, and is not easily applicable to small companies with a few branches and a brief history of sales. Deep neural networks, too, require much training data and careful tuning and the paper doesn't talk about the cost of maintaining those types of networks in a small business.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper justifies the selection of learning-based approach for demand forecasting instead of manual estimation as it is the initial step of StockSense. This is relevant to StockSense, which also relies on product attributes as predictive variables, as it describes each product by category, size and colour, which are used as features. The concept of propagating information between similar products, based on their attributes, is particularly beneficial for items with a short history because StockSense has to predict demand for each product, size and store combination. Meanwhile, the constraints of the paper indicate that StockSense is better suited to using gradient-boosted trees instead of deep networks, which require a lot of data – one that is hard for a small retailer to provide. But the paper also reminds us that expert knowledge is still useful, as StockSense allows owners to "view" and "reject" every recommendation rather than implementing them all at once.

\subsection{Clearance Pricing for Fast Fashion}

This subsection summarizes the article ``Clearance pricing optimization for a fast-fashion retailer'' by Caro and Gallien (2012). This article was selected because it is one of the few measures of markdown optimization that has been practically applied and tested in the field. It is summarized, critically analyzed and compared with StockSense in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

The fast-fashion retailer Zara used the end-of-season process of making markdowns by hand, which Caro and Gallien~\cite{caro2012clearance} describe as a process which can be replaced by a forecast-and-optimize process. The process involves a demand forecasting model to estimate how the sales of each article react to a change in article price and an optimization model to choose the price of each article for the next few weeks of the clearance period. The authors tried the new process in a controlled field experiment in all stores in Belgium and Ireland in a fall-winter clearing season. The results of the experiment revealed that new process led to an increase of about 6% in the clearance revenue, compared with the manual process. The following result convinced the company to follow this process in all their stores worldwide. The process was designed so that it would fit into the practical constraints of the company, including a finite number of permitted prices and a current price change program. This demonstrates that a formal model can be integrated into a process that has already been in place in a retail setting without interfering with the retail process.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

This paper is a rare documented case of a markdown optimization model that has been tested and implemented in the field and proven to impact revenue at scale. The field experiment is more evidence than a simulation and the paper makes the explanation clear of the integration of the model into the existing decision process of the company. The problem with its use for StockSense is that its developed for an extremely large and integrated retailer that has historical pricing for thousands of articles. This pricing history is not maintained by most small retailers, making the forecasting of the response to discounts difficult. It's also geared towards the end of the season, when it's all about turning stock, not preventing idle stock from accumulating during the season.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper validates the core concept of StockSense's markdown optimizer: a structured methodology that integrates forecasting and pricing is more effective than relying on instinctive manual decisions to make markdowns. StockSense, like the other approaches, is organized around forecast then optimize, with demand being forecast first and the smallest discount, which hits the sales target, being chosen. The difference is that StockSense takes the concept and makes it more applicable to small amounts of data and uses it to act as a warning bell in the season, instead of a clearance drill at the end of the season. If the tenant's price data is not sufficiently varied, StockSense employs a simple price elasticity model with a conservative category level estimate. The outcome of the Zara experiment also provides a realistic estimate about the amount of benefit, which can assist in assessing the outcome of the backtest of the StockSense.

\subsection{Lateral Transshipment Models}

This subsection covers the article, ``Inventory models with lateral transshipments: A review'' by Paterson, Kiesm\"uller, Teunter and Glazebrook (2011) in the European Journal of Operational Research. To facilitate the distribution of the article, the research into moving stock between locations has been organised, and the article was selected for this purpose. It is summarized and critically analysed in the following subsections and is related to StockSense.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Paterson, Kiesm\"uller, Teunter and Glazebrook~\cite{paterson2011inventory} summarize the academic research that has been conducted on lateral transshipments, those involving a flow of stock between nodes at the same tier of a supply chain, for example between retail outlets. The reviews categorise the research by the time of transfers. Reactive transshipments occur because of a stockout or a forecasted stockout at one location; proactive transshipments occur when inventory is planned in advance to rebalance a stockout at one location to the other. The authors also categorize the models according to their characteristics (number of locations, stock review policy, cost structure) and present the key results found for each class of model. The review ends with the identification of some research questions that remain open in the area. The key takeaway for a retailer from the review is that timing and transfer conditions are as important, or more so, than the decision to undertake a transfer. In the case of proactive policies, transshipment is directly related to demand forecasting, since a forecast is necessary to determine where the demand will occur.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

To summarise, the paper provides a wide and well-structured overview of transshipment models, and facilitates the identification of which sets of assumptions have been investigated and which have not been. The fact that it distinguishes between reactive and proactive transfers is helpful, as it can be used to build a recommendation system. The paper, however, does not suggest a particular algorithm that would work for a small retailer with only two or three branches, and most of the models that it discusses make assumptions about the detail of demand distributions and cost that small businesses don't have. The review also doesn't cover the question of what to do with a transfer, when there may be other actions that can be taken, like a markdown or ordering new stock for the same calendar year.When there's multiple actions that can be taken, such as a markdown or new orders for the same calendar year, the review does not cover how they should be combined with a transfer. The gaps may be justified for a review of theoretical models, but they indicate that a practical system will need to blend the concepts presented in the review with reduced and more powerful decision rules.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

This review is academically grounded, and treats transfers between branches as a distinct choice from reordering and discounting. The proactive framing described in the paper is the foundation of StockSense since its distribution suggestions are created to stop dead stock and stockout before it occurs. Each transfer decision is made as a small transportation problem, moving surplus units to those branches that are forecast to be short and for which the expected margin is greater than the transportation cost of the transfer. StockSense also tries to solve the problems mentioned above by sequencing the way the stock is redistributed, instead of markdowns and reorders, meaning that existing stock is redistributed first and not discounts or new orders. This way the review contributes to the way the recommendation is formulated and also to the place of the redistribution in the overall recommendation process.

\subsection{Forecasting Groups of Similar Series}

This subsection is a review of the article "Forecasting across time series databases using recurrent neural networks on groups of similar series: A clustering approach" by Bandara, Bergmeir and Smyl, published in 2020 in Expert Systems with Applications. The article is selected because it demonstrates the use of one model to train as a group a set of similar series, each of which is short. Its summary, critical analysis and relationship to StockSense are given in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Bandara, Bergmeir and Smyl~\cite{bandara2020forecasting} suggest a forecasting model for a large number of related time series. They do not train one model to each series, but rather group the series into clusters of similar series, and train a single recurrent neural network for all series in a cluster. The idea is that series with similar patterns can share information, in turn, with the other series in the same cluster, so that a short series can benefit from the history of other series in the same cluster. The authors compare the method with the standard univariate methods and with a number of benchmark datasets from forecasting competitions and demonstrate that learning on clusters of related series leads to better accuracy than learning a single model on all series or using several standard univariate forecasting methods. The method is therefore based on two concepts that have since become prevalent in large scale forecasting: group similar series and train a shared model. The authors also explore the possibility of grouping the series into groups based on any specific characteristics of the series, such as trend and seasonality.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

Many short or sparse series, as is usual in retail sales at detailed level, is well suited to the approach, and it is clearly shown in the paper that the sharing of information between series can enhance accuracy. The clustering step also provides a systematic approach to handling extremely distinct sets of series within the same dataset. The biggest drawback is that the method was applied primarily to benchmark time series from competitions, instead of retail sales at product, size, and store level, which have a high percentage of zeros and are influenced by prices and promotions. Recurrent neural networks, on the other hand, require fine tuning and a lot of training data – which can be tricky to get for each individual tenant of a platform serving small businesses. The additional clustering step also introduces a design parameter, the number of clusters, which is a design parameter that has to be optimized for each new dataset.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper argues the benefit of training a single forecasting model for a large number of product, size and store series, as opposed to a separate model per product, size, and store. If there are too few observations in each individual series to train a reliable local model by a small retailer, data from various series must be shared to get started. What StockSense does is the same as clustering but in a simpler manner: By providing a gradient-boosted model with the product, size, store and category as features, the model can learn the behavior of series with similar products. This preserves the benefits of global models, yet the training requirements of the recurrent neural network are reduced. The clustering idea in the paper might be implemented later in the experiments of Chapter~9, if it turned out that there are different groups of series that behave differently. The paper hence gives reasons for the design in the present state and suggests a possible improvement of the design.

\subsection{Results of the M5 Competition}

We read the article “M5 accuracy competition: Results, findings, and conclusions” by Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022) in the International Journal of Forecasting.We read the article “M5 accuracy competition: Results, findings, and conclusions” by Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022) in the International Journal of Forecasting. The article was chosen because it is one of the largest comparisons of forecasting methods on real retail sales data. It is summarized and analyzed critically in the following subsections and also shown its relationship with StockSense.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

The results of the M5 forecasting competition are reported by Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos~\cite{makridakis2022m5} with thousands of people competing to predict the unit sales of Walmart products. This dataset consisted of 42,840 series, hierarchically organised around products, departments, categories, stores/state and prices, promotions and calendar information. The competition concluded that ML techniques (and particularly gradient boosted trees like LightGBM) had better accuracy than the statistical benchmarks for this type of data. The majority of the winning solutions used multiple series for training a global model and external information like prices or calendar events. The authors find that these two factors, namely cross-learning across series and explanatory variables, were important contributors to the improved accuracy. There was also an uncertainty track in the competition, where participants predicted quantiles of the demand, highlighting the importance of measuring the dispersion of the possible demands and not just the mean.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

One of the biggest empirical comparisons of forecasting methods based on real retail data is the M5, and the results of this competition are widely cited to evaluate the performance of model families for retail sales. It is strong for the size and realism of the data set, and the number of independent participants which ensures robust findings. But this data is derived from a large retailer with many years of sales in history for thousands of products and the competition doesn't think about the situation of small retailers with short sales history. It also excludes sales by size or variant — and it's a key feature of StockSense. The solutions that won tended to be quite elaborate, optimized for the competition, and thus were not necessarily a template for a small business platform; rather, they represented a family of models.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

Directly influencing the choice of LightGBM as the primary forecasting model for StockSense, the finding of gradient-boosted trees outperforming statistical methods with hierarchical retail sales data. The competition also includes two other design decisions: training a single global model over all the series of a tenant and adding price and calendar features. Simultaneously, its shortcomings make it very clear that StockSense cannot sacrifice its simplicity even with simple statistics, and that it needs to test out experiments only with short histories, as the gains of gradient boosting on huge amounts of data from a large retailer will have to be proven in a much more limited and ephemeral data set for small businesses. The preliminary experiments outlined in Chapter~9 will also make use of the M5 dataset. The uncertainty results of the competition also validate the use of the upper quantile forecast for the input to the safety stock application in the recommendation engine. The findings of the M5 impact the model, the features and the treatment of uncertainty in StockSense in this way.

\subsection{Forecasting for Small Businesses}

In this subsection, the article titled ``Demand forecasting for improved inventory management in small and medium-sized businesses'' from Purnamasari et al., (2023) in Jurnal Nasional Pendidikan Teknik Informatika is reviewed. \subsection{Global and Local Forecasting Models}

In this subsection we discuss the paper ``Principles and algorithms for forecasting groups of time series: Locality and globality'' by Montero-Manso and Hyndman (2021) published in the International Journal of Forecasting. This article was selected for its explanation of scenarios where a common sensical global model is a better predictor than individual local models. It is summarized and critically analysed and its relationship to StockSense is presented in the next subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Montero-Manso and Hyndman~\cite{montero2021principles} investigate when a global model which is shared by a set of related time series is more useful than individual local models for each series. They provide theoretical arguments: (1) models can be as expressive as local ones, and (2) that their complexity can grow with the size of the data set without overfitting any of the series. They illustrate these claims with experiments on large numbers of time series, demonstrating that in some cases global models can be more successful than local models, even when there is no apparent similarity between the time series. The authors also talk about the selection of complexity of global models in practice. They also demonstrate how to add features and nonlinear techniques to global models, such as tree-based models and neural networks, and retain these benefits. This allows their results to be used directly in the retail forecasting models that use gradient-boosted models.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

The paper provides a useful theoretical and empirical basis for global forecasting models, and the findings of the paper have already been applied in projects with numerous short series, which are applied to justify model selection. One strength is that it gives an explanation for the effectiveness of global models – it does not just demonstrate the effectiveness of models. The paper is a general time series study, however, and the recommendations must be modified to reflect the structure of product, size, and store sales data, which feature a large number of zeros and calendar effects and price effects. The experiments also do not take into account the scenario where data from different organizations need to be kept in different places, thus restricting the scale of a global model that can be shared. Although it has its drawbacks, the paper is one of the clearer explanations of the utility of the global approach, and the framework is increasingly used with growing frequency in practice.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

This finding is in line with the StockSense decision to adopt one shared forecasting model for all product, size and store levels of a tenant as opposed to having a model for each series, as this would require a much longer history of data than a small retailer would have. Unlike the global model idea, StockSense applies the global model idea within each tenant and not across tenants since tenant data is not supposed to be shared with other businesses. One of the experiments planned in Chapter~9 is to determine whether this per-tenant global model is more accurate than separate local models. A global model is also lower cost to maintain, as only a single model needs to be trained and stored on the platform – not one for each product, size and store. This results in a low cost forecasting service and makes it affordable for small businesses.

\subsection{Accurate Response to Early Sales}

This subsection looks at the article ``Reducing the cost of demand uncertainty through accurate response to early sales,'' by Fisher & Raman (1996), published in Operations Research. The article was selected because it illustrates the importance of responding swiftly to early sales cues when making decisions about stock. Its summary, critical analysis and relationship to StockSense are given in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Fisher and Raman~\cite{fisher1996reducing} demonstrate that fashion companies can cut down the demand uncertainty costs by decreasing lead time and by responding to the sales of the initial season rather than making all the production and ordering decisions months ahead. They build a model that can take into account the initial sales data to adjust the forecast of demand, as well as to determine the amount of each product to produce after the first sale is known. It was used at a large ski-wear firm and the authors estimate that it could boost profits by about 60% over the company's informal response system. It is demonstrated in the paper that a lot of the uncertainty of fashion demand can be eliminated by monitoring a fraction of the initial sales. The method used to cross this is called "accurate response" and has had a significant impact on the subsequent research of quick response in the fashion sector.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

This is the classic paper and it has clearly demonstrated the importance of reacting quickly to demand signals as opposed to making instinctive decisions on stocking levels. It's best when it has a simple decision model, coupled with a practical application and a simple idea of the monetary gain. But it is about the production and ordering of a single season, a single manufacturer and retailer, and that is very different from what a small multi-branch shop is involved with on a day to day basis. The model also assumes that production capacity can be reserved and scaled up or down within the season (this is not pertinent for retailers who purchase finished goods from suppliers). The paper is also older than the modern ML methods used to forecast today, thus its forecasting aspect is less complicated than today's practice. The contribution of its main part, therefore, is not so much the forecasting method itself, but the decision principle.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper gives an early foundation to the concept of which StockSense is based on: quick response to demand signals leads to lower stock-out and over-stocking. It can be used to recommend and optimise markdowns, considering recent sales velocity as an important signal. In StockSense, this concept is reflected in two forecasting model features: lag and rolling, which ensure that recent weeks have a major impact on the forecast; and the markdown optimizer, which signals when an item will be sold out before its weeks of cover have concluded, rather than at the end of the season. The two components of the system process new information as soon as it becomes available, rather than at a predetermined planning time. For small retailers, who do not have the luxury of big safety stocks, this is particularly relevant: they need to be responsive to the signals that they receive in the sale.

The review offers advice regarding the evaluation of forecasts and the choice of model that impacts the comparison of the gradient-boosted model with the statistical benchmarks used in StockSense. It also allows the use of chronological data splits, rolling-origin validation and error measures that are suitable for sparse series, such as weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE), weighted mean absolute error (WMAE), mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) (in addition to the root mean squared error (RMSE)). These options are explained in the forecast evaluation methodology, presented in Chapter~5, and in the experiments to be carried out, presented in Chapter~9. It also enables results to be reported separately for series of each type, as a good performance on the series that matters most is offset by a bad performance on the one that didn't. These practices enable a fair comparison of LightGBM and the baselines in StockSense, and can help ensure that the results can be believed.

\subsection{Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics}

This subsection summarizes the article “From predictive to prescriptive analytics” [2] of Bertsimas and Kallus (2020), published in Management Science. The article is selected because it provides an explanation of how predictions can be converted into decisions that reduce the cost. The following subsections present a summary, critical analysis and comparison of it with StockSense.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Bertsimas and Kallus (2020) provide a prescription framework for converting predictive ML models into prescriptions (decisions) that reduce an expected cost function, Bertsimas and Kallus~\cite{bertsimas2020predictive}. Their method differs from first predicting a value and then optimizing the value as if it is correct, by weighting the past observations that are similar to the current situation and then optimizing directly for that weighted data. The authors illustrate the framework with an inventory management problem at a large media distribution company and demonstrate that it provides superior decisions over methods that don't consider the relationship between prediction and decision. It also sets a benchmark on the value of the data that can be measured on the extent to which the decisions are better because it is integrated within the framework. This paper is very significant as a link between ML and Operations Research.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

The paper's theoretical foundations are solid enough to take them past the prediction phase to the decision phase, something that is key to the design of StockSense. It is its primary role to demonstrate that the quality of a decision is not only given by the accuracy of the prediction, but also by its use. The case study is about a large distribution network, with many locations and a lot of auxiliary data, while StockSense is about retailers with a few branches and limited data. The methods are also more complex to implement and explain than the simple decision rules that small business owners can easily follow. So there is a balance to be struck between quality of the decisions and transparency and StockSense strikes it in favour of transparency because small business owners need to know the advice that they are getting. The main idea of the paper could still be used in this simpler context.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper reinforces the StockSense design choice of providing the user with a set of concrete recommendations – either a redistribution or markdown recommendation or an order recommendation – rather than just a prediction of demand. Similarly, StockSense works in a simpler way, by incorporating an upper quantile forecast and a safety stock, and selecting transfers and discounts to maximize the expected margin, while taking into consideration the uncertainty about the forecast. This ensures that the decision logic is clear enough to be described to the owner and that each decision is associated with the forecast. The historical back test that is scheduled in Chapter~9 most closely follows the spirit of the paper since it rates the system not only on how accurate its forecasts are, but dead stock and lost sales as well. The methods presented in the paper could aid in the optimization of the decision rules after the accumulation of sufficient data in future work.

\subsection{Interpretable Models for High-Stakes Decisions}

We discuss the article ``Stop explaining black box machine learning models for high stakes decisions and use interpretable models instead,'' in Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019, by Rudin. The article was selected because it explores when the predictions from a model can be relied upon and how they are to be interpreted. Its summary, critical analysis and relationship to StockSense are given in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

When it comes to high-stakes decisions, it is better to use “interpretable by design” models rather than “black box” models that are only explained later, says Rudin~\cite{rudin2019stop}. The paper points out that explanations generated for black box models may be unreliable or misleading as they only approximate the actual behavior of the model. It provides several examples from the criminal justice and healthcare settings that illustrate how interpretative models can be as accurate as black box models, and encourages researchers and practitioners to prefer an interpretative model when outcomes have lasting implications on people. She also states that it's not always necessary to sacrifice accuracy to gain interpretability, particularly when it comes to structured data that has meaningful features. The paper is one of the most influential papers in the debate around explainable artificial intelligence (AI).

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

The argument is well supported, cited, and widely accepted, and has helped shape the thinking on explainability within the research community. It has convincing examples for decisions that impact human lives. Rather than being centered on retail inventory decisions, where the stakes of a particular recommendation are relatively small and perhaps some loss of interpretability is acceptable in the trade for accuracy, the paper is rather centered around high-stakes domains such as justice and medicine, where the consequences of a wrong decision are very high. The paper does not also cover cases where the users of a model do not have expertise, such as small business owners, for whom an interpretable model might still have to be rendered in a plain language. Its message does not, however, hold true for any system whose outputs people are expected to act on.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The problem is the same in StockSense because recommendations are not as dire as Rudin says; the business owner isn't going to do something if he doesn't understand it. This helps to reinforce the StockSense philosophy of providing an explanation for each and every recommendation. In order to minimize the risk introduced by Rudin, the bulk of each StockSense explanation is actually the quantity that the decision rule uses, for instance, forecast demand, current stock and transfer cost, and only the description of the forecast drivers is included from the model's feature contributions. The decision rules, like the transportation problem and the markdown grid are straightforward and clear, and can be explained exactly, not approximately.

\subsection{Study of Retail Out-of-Stocks}

The article by Corsten and Gruen (2003) ``Desperately seeking shelf availability: An examination of the extent, the causes, and the efforts to address retail out-of-stocks'' is reviewed in this subsection. The article was chosen because it measures how common and how costly stockouts are for retailers. Its summary, critical analysis and relationship to StockSense are given in the following subsections.

\subsubsection{Summary of the Research Item}

Corsten and Gruen~\cite{corsten2003desperately} examine the extent, causes and consequences of out-of-stocks in retail based on a large set of studies conducted worldwide. They say that the out-of-stock figure had not got better in the long-term, and they found that on average about eight per cent of products were not in stock at any given time. Problems with store ordering and replenishment rather than lack of suppliers' availability were found to be responsible for the majority of out-of-stocks. The paper also examines the consumer response to an out-of-stock product, and demonstrates that a significant portion of the consumers purchase the product at another store, rather than choosing an alternative product in the same store, or postponing the purchase or refusing to buy altogether.

\subsubsection{Critical Analysis of the Research Item}

This study is one of the most important and frequently cited studies in the literature that demonstrates that out-of-stocks is a persistent retail problem and that it is very costly for a retailer; it is therefore a more practical than theoretical motivation for this project. The findings on the causes of out-of-stocks are particularly helpful, as it demonstrates that the majority of out-of-stocks can be solved by making better ordering decisions at the store level. The biggest drawback to StockSense is that it deals with fast moving consumer goods as opposed to apparel and footwear, which is more seasonal, and which has an additional dimension in the form of sizes. Subsequent research has confirmed similar out-of-stock rates and therefore the major findings of this research are still relevant.

\subsubsection{Relationship to the Proposed Research Work}

The paper builds on the business argument for the importance of giving priority to redistribution and timely reordering. The impact of a stockout is greater than the loss of one item would seem to indicate because many customers who experience a stockout don't purchase anything at that store. It's finding that most of the stockouts occur at the store level also helps to support StockSense's approach of predicting demand on a per-store basis and then stocking that store in line with its demand, instead of ordering based on an average across all stores. It also provides explanation as to why lost sales are included in the StockSense backtest with dead stock.

\section{Related Work}

This section provides an overview of six commercial platforms that address the need for demand planning or multi-location inventory management. They vary from enterprise planning suites created for big chains to point-of-sale systems created for small retail establishments. The review details the key features of each platform and provides an explanation of the differences between each platform and StockSense, particularly in terms of cost, forecast detail, and the importance of redistributing between stores. The information is from the public product pages of each vendor. The majority of the most common types of solutions that retailers will use today have been featured in the selection, making it easily understood how StockSense fits into the marketplace.

\subsection{SAP Integrated Business Planning}

SAP Integrated Business Planning (IBP) is an enterprise demand sensing and supply chain planning platform~\cite{sapibp}. It ingests real time signal data such as POS data, to adjust short-term predictions, and to prevent out-of-stock and overstocking situations in big companies' supply chains. It also provides sales and operations planning as well as inventory optimization and supply planning, all in one, in a single integrated solution that plug-and-play's into the company's ERP system. The platform is intended for large organizations that have their own planning teams and an ERP system, and that have enterprise licensing budgets. Two or three stores is too many for a small retailer to afford the cost of licensing, implementation and specialist staff, and its application is much broader than a small store could ever require. StockSense solves the same key issue – matching stocks to demand – and is offered as a free point-of-sale application for a small business that can be extended through a subscription for an affordable price.

\subsection{Blue Yonder}

Large retailers use a Panasonic subsidiary, Blue Yonder, to leverage ML for demand planning and retail planning software.Retail planning software and demand planning software based on ML, are provided by Blue Yonder, a Panasonic company, used by large retailers. Its forecasting is more than the historical average, it is based on a model of the causal influences on demand, including prices, promotions, weather and events; and its forecasting is done in a manner that makes the planner aware of the reasons behind a forecast. These are good qualities and in particular, the explanation of forecasts is similar in spirit to the StockSense approach. But as with SAP, Blue Yonder is an enterprise application that would take a lot of work to implement, integrate with current systems and depends on a technical infrastructure that small companies don't have. It focuses its products and implementation on enterprise retailers. StockSense is the name of the idea of the explained, causally informed forecast and brings it in a far more simple package, appropriate to a small retailer.

\subsection{Nextail}

Nextail is a merchandising platform based on AI that is designed specifically for fashion, footwear and apparel retailers~\cite{nextail}. It enables in-season reallocation, replenishment and rebalancing of inventory to stores to maximize full price sales and minimize end-of-season mark-downs. Nextail is the closest to redistribution focus of StockSense since it explicitly moves stock between stores based on demand. It's also detailed, as a critical feature of apparel with numerous sizes. It does, however, have a focus on mid-size and large fashion chains that have numerous stores, costs significantly more than a small retailer can afford and provides its suggestions as part of a greater merchandising suite. There is no free entry point like a POS, which means that a small store would need to pay up for and implement the entire platform for any benefit to be gained.

\subsection{Shopify POS (with Stocky)}

Shopify POS provides multi-location inventory management, stocks transfers between locations and low-stock notifications~\cite{shopifypos}. It also has a Stocky app along with its Sidekick AI assistant, which offer demand-based reorder suggestions and rebalancing suggestions between stores. It is the most accessible of the tools reviewed for a small retailer, especially for a small business that has an online presence via Shopify. The advanced inventory features, however, are not available in basic, but only in higher paid plans, and cost a lot of money per month if you have the entire suite of features. Moreover, its forecasting and rebalancing efforts are primarily at the product level, not at the size/variant level, as it is at those levels that most dead stock accumulates for apparel and footwear. StockSense is different because it predicts the size/location level and because it prioritizes the redistribution of trades over new trades.

\subsection{Lightspeed Retail}

Lightspeed Retail is a point of sale (POS) and inventory system for retailers with multiple stores and many SKUs (stock keeping units)~\cite{lightspeed}. It enables stocks to be moved between stores, offers individual reorder points for each store and reports on slow moving stock, helping you control branch stocks. The rules, however, are reorder points that the user needs to establish and the slow movers report items only after they have already had poor sales. The platform does not provide demand forecasting at the size or variant level, explanations for its suggestions or a separate tool for choosing the timing of markdowns. It also lacks a free version, meaning that retailers will have to pay for the POS even if they don't require more sophisticated inventory analytics capabilities. The difference with StockSense is that it provides the POS free of charge and that it replaces the manual reorder points and past reports with forecasting and timely suggestions.

\subsection{Odoo}

Odoo is an open-source business platform, the free Community version of which contains a POS application and an Inventory application that has reordering rules and transfers between warehouses~\cite{odoo}. It is the one closest to the free POS model of StockSense as all the core POS and stock management functions are free. Yet the free version doesn't offer any built-in demand forecasting, there's no analysis at the level of size and variant, and no explained recommendations. However these are only offered as add-on marketplace apps, at an additional fee, or in the paid-for Enterprise version, and add-on apps typically do not predict at the branch level and do not prioritize redistribution between branches. It simply requires some technical work to set up and maintain Odoo, and many small shop owners can't do that for themselves. StockSense is a free POS system that combines with the built-in forecasting and recommendations specifically for small multi-branch retailers.

\section{Conclusion}

The literature demonstrates the benefits of ML forecasting and formal markdown and redistribution approaches, specifically, benefits on inventory decisions and revenue. Research into the M5 competition and small-business data reveals that gradient-boosted models that are trained on a large number of related series offer a better way to predict retail demand than statistical approaches, and research on transshipments and markdowns demonstrates the value of proactively moving stock and discounting it in a controlled manner. Most of the research, and much of the commercial solutions mentioned, are geared toward bigger retailers with the capacity to implement them, however. No solution in the field of point of sale was found to integrate a free POS, explained forecasts and recommendations at branch, size and location level, with first priority being given to small multi-branch retailers' needs for redistribution. StockSense is created to fill this void.

\section{Selected Model}

Based on this review, the StockSense forecasting engine will use the gradient-boosted tree family, implemented with LightGBM, as its main model, following the M5 results. One global model will be trained for each tenant for all product, size and store series and simple statistical methods used as baselines. Recommendations for redistribution will be based on the proactive transshipment framework of Paterson et al., where lower priority will be placed on the discounting and reordering of moving stock. The markdown optimizer will be used in the same way as Caro and Gallien suggest – forecast then optimize – but will be implemented early in the season to allow items to be marked down before they become unsellable. After Rudin, each recommendation will be explained.

\section{Quality Attributes}

The quality attributes below describe the characteristics that the design of StockSense must have in order to be dependable, easy to change and easy to use. They complement the functional requirements and guide the design decisions in Chapter~6.

\subsection{Reliability}

The system shall give consistent forecasts and recommendations from the same data, and two runs of the same sales history shall give the same results. This is important because owners will evaluate recommendations over time, and they will lose confidence in the system if the treatment recommendation does not change as the data changes. For this, all the forecasts are saved with the name of the model that generated them, and fixed random seeds are used in model training. The system shall also gracefully reject invalid uploads. A file with missing columns, invalid dates or negative quantities must never crash the server or corrupt the data that a tenant has already saved. Validating uploaded rows before anything is written into the database, and importing the valid rows in a single transaction which is rolled back entirely when any part of the import operation fails. If the forecasting run doesn't work, the previous run with valid forecasts will stay on the dashboard until the next forecast is successful.

\subsection{Maintainability}

The forecasting, recommendation, markdown and POS logic shall be built as separate modules with clear interfaces, so that any one of them can be changed, retrained or replaced without affecting the others. For instance, the forecasting model can be changed to a different algorithm, without changing any other components of the system, provided that the new algorithm writes the same forecast table. This separation also allows the team members to work on different modules in parallel during the second phase of the project. The code will be: Organized in one package per module, will be reviewed by another team member prior to merging, will use agreed upon naming conventions. Errors such as failed uploads, rejected rows and failed forecasting runs shall be logged with enough detail to find their cause quickly, which keeps the effort of maintaining the system low after deployment.

\subsection{Usability}

The dashboard should deliver forecasts and recommendations in a way that would be easily understood by business owners or store managers with no data science or ML knowledge. Forecasts will be displayed in charts next to the history of sales and every recommendation will be worded in a short sentence, avoiding complex business jargon, and will include what to do, why, and what the financial impact will be. Ordinary users won't be shown the statistical terms like quantiles or error metrics. The POS screen needs to be used during busy business hours and needs to be able to record a sale in only a few clicks. When an uploaded file fails to read, for example, error messages should describe in a simple manner what went wrong and how to resolve the issue. The interface will be tested with first-time users to see if they can register, record a sale and act on a recommendation without help or a user manual.

\subsection{Correctness}

The inventory quantities shown to users shall always reflect the latest sales, adjustments and completed transfers. All sales made using the POS will decrease the stock of the sold variant in the same database transaction that saves the sale, meaning that there will never be any discrepancies between the inventory and the sale record. Any manual adjustments following a physical count are recorded with a reason and accepted redistribution recommendations update the count for both stores when the transfer is done. Recommendations must be based on the stock position at the time recommendations are generated, if the stock changes at the source store before the transfer is approved, the system should alert the user and not generate a transfer that will not be valid. Also, the correctness of the input data is important in the making of forecasts and recommendations, and uploaded data is therefore validated before it's accepted, against the products, sizes and stores of the tenant.

\subsection{Efficiency}

System should generate recommendations and forecasts for the full catalog of a tenant's products within a reasonable time, based on the size of the data set that is uploaded. The training of models, the generation of forecasts and the processing of large uploads are executed in background processes and the outcomes are saved in the database, so that they are not calculated again when the dashboard is opened. Reads uploaded CSV files in chunks and reads Excel files in batches, and that helps to maintain the low memory consumption even while reading files with up to 100,000 rows. The recommendation problems are small, because each one involves only the few branches of a single business, so they can be solved quickly for every variant. Almost all of the queries will be using tenant, store and variant identifiers, which will be indexed on database tables.

\subsection{Flexibility}

The system should enable new forecasting models to be added without redesigning the data ingestion or dashboard layers and also add new recommendation rules without redesigning the data ingestion or dashboard layers. Each forecast is kept with the model's name, so multiple models may be compared side-by-side, and one of them selected for a tenant. The recommendation engine runs based on forecasts and stock positions and does not rely on a specific model, so new rules can be added as the next steps in the engine's pipeline, such as for seasonal promotions. The data ingestion layer maps uploaded columns to a common schema, so support for the file formats of further POS systems can be added by defining new mappings rather than changing the rest of the system. The forecasting is also general purpose - it can be used for a business that carries any product that is in variants, sizes and/or multiple locations rather than just clothing and footwear.

\section{Non-Functional Requirements}

The non-functional requirements are below, and they are measurable requirements for performance, reliability, security and compatibility. These targets are checked by the non-functional test cases in Chapter~7.

\subsection{Performance}

The system should be able to accept a typical small retailer's monthly sales upload (a few thousand rows) and generate updated forecasts in a couple of minutes. An historical upload of up to 100,000 rows should be processed in less than 5 minutes and will not cause the system to run out of memory. Since the POS is used while the customer is waiting at the counter, a sale recorded in the POS should be saved within 2 seconds along with the inventory update. The dashboard pages should load fast as they display the forecasts and recommendations that have been computed and stored in advance, and should not be computed on request. Forecasting and model training are background jobs and training one tenant should not affect the performance of the POS/dashboards for other tenants, especially if it is a lengthy training run. These targets will be tested with relevant tenant data on realistic data sets, for tenants having 2 and 3 branches.

\subsection{Reliability}

The POS and the dashboard will be highly available as the store managers depend on the POS for recording sales throughout the day, and any disruption implies sales not recorded. The system shall thus be implemented in such a way that if there is any failure within the forecasting or the uploading part, it cannot disrupt the operation of the POS while also making sure that the failed forecasting job is repeated automatically. The database will be periodically backed up so as to allow recovery of the business data in case of any failure. The failure of the upload or the forecasting must ensure that the previous data and forecasts remain unchanged, something that will be ensured by using database transactions and keeping the old forecasts until new ones are generated successfully.

\subsection{Security}

The tenant data will be isolated at the database level in such a way that all business tables will have a tenant id which is checked by the back end before any read/write operations. Access within a tenant will be role-based such that the store manager will only be able to view and modify the data of the allotted store while the system administrator will be able to manage tenants and plans without being able to view the sales details of those tenants unless required for support purposes. Passwords will only be stored in form of hashes made using an encryption algorithm and not in plaintext. All communication from the browser to the server will take place using Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS) over TLS and the server will not accept any plain HTTP connection requests. Database queries will be parameterized so that there cannot be any form of injection attacks and secret keys will be kept separate from the source code.

\subsection{Compatibility}

The StockSense application would be available via a browser such as Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari, so there would be no need to install any software on the computers of the users. This is important since small businesses are usually have old laptops and desktops, as well as tablet computers, at the point of sale. The interface of the StockSense would be responsive to enable usage of the dashboard as well as the POS system on various sized screens, including tablets. For data import, the system would accept CSV and Excel files, which could be exported by practically all POS systems and spreadsheet applications. The user would be able to map the columns in the file to necessary fields and pick the date format.

\section{Assumptions}

The requirements above are based on several assumptions about the businesses that will use StockSense and the environment in which they work. If any of these assumptions does not hold for a particular business, some features, especially forecasting, may be less effective for that business. The assumptions are listed below. The assumptions will be reviewed during testing, and the requirements will be adjusted if any of them proves unrealistic for the target businesses.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Businesses have, or are willing to record, sales data at the product, size and store level.
    \item Users have basic computer skills and a stable internet connection.
    \item Uploaded CSV and Excel files follow, or can be mapped to, a reasonably consistent column structure.
    \item Each store belongs to exactly one tenant at a time.
    \item Forecast accuracy for a tenant will improve as more historical data accumulates, which will be measured rather than assumed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Hardware and Software Requirements}

This section lists the hardware needed to host and use StockSense and the software stack with which it will be built. The stack follows the model choice made in Chapter~3 and the architecture described in Chapter~6.

\subsection{Hardware Requirements}

StockSense will operate on a server, while clients would just need to have a computing device with a browser. The minimum requirements for the server would be 8 GB RAM and quad-core processor. This is enough to host the web application, the database and background jobs for model training and file uploads for several tenants during development and early stages of operation. The server can be located in the cloud or locally. Also, server resources can be scaled up if the number of tenants and volume of sales history grows. For the client side, any modern PC, tablet or smartphone with a web browser and internet access would be enough, since everything is happening on the server. There is no need for any specialized equipment like barcode scanners and receipt printers, but the POS interface is created in a way that allows such equipment to be integrated into the system in the future.

\subsection{Software Requirements}

The backend of StockSense will be implemented using Python along with the FastAPI framework, providing the API interface used by the web dashboard and POS screens, as well as forecasting and recommendations service. The web dashboard and POS interface will be developed using React, allowing users to work fully in the browser. All business-related data will be kept in the PostgreSQL relational database. The forecasting will utilize scikit-learn library for the data preprocessing and evaluation, and LightGBM library for gradient boosted tree models, as suggested based on the results of M5 Competition in Chapter~3, while the statistical baselines will be computed using statsmodels library. Pandas and openpyxl will be used to process uploaded CSV and Excel files. The server will be running in the Linux environment, and version control will be provided by Git and GitHub.

\section{Database Design}

All business data in StockSense is stored in one PostgreSQL database used by all tenants, where each business table is marked by its tenant ID. This architecture ensures that the data belonging to each business remains logically isolated but the platform is able to efficiently support many small tenants on the same infrastructure and at a low price. The database architecture is based on the concept of the variant and the store, since all the sales, inventories, forecasting, and recommendations are registered on this level.

\subsection{ER Diagram}

Figure~\ref{fig:er} presents the ER diagram of StockSense. Every tenant has its users, stores, and products, meaning that all business information is owned by exactly one tenant. A product has multiple variants, e.g., different sizes, and the inventory entity contains information about the number of units of each variant stored at each store. Sales information is stored for a particular variant at a particular store on a certain date along with a number of items sold and whether it was made through the POS system or in a file upload. Historical sales populate the forecast entity, which includes the predicted demand of each variant at each store within a weekly or monthly period. In turn, forecasts are used to populate the recommendation entity, which consists of redistribution, reorder and markdown recommendations along with explanations, estimated impact, and status along with the reference to the store(s). Redistribution recommendations are associated with two stores: the source store, which has excess stock, and the target store, which has a shortage in forecast, whereas reorder and markdown recommendations are associated with only one store.

\section{Risk Analysis}

There are several possible risks that might influence the successful implementation of StockSense, and all of them have been addressed while designing the solution. First of all, there is a technical risk connected to the fact that the forecasts might be inaccurate for a very low number of transactions in tenants' sales history. It can be solved by training a single global model for all series of a tenant, by showing forecasts based on a model only when the history is larger than a certain minimum threshold and by providing simple baselines for comparison. Another related risk is the low quality of uploaded sales reports that might cause inconsistencies in the dataset and require a lot of manual effort in order to clean the dataset and provide incorrect forecasts. Invalidation and upload report from Chapter~5 are designed to solve this problem by rejecting invalid rows along with detailed explanations of their errors before importing any data. Moreover, there is a risk of low adoption of the tool due to the lack of previous experience of small business owners with automated recommendations; therefore, explanation of the recommendations and estimates of their impact are provided. There is also a scalability risk connected as more tenants and more historical data accumulate, since forecasting jobs must remain fast enough, so training runs in background jobs and results are cached. Finally, there are project risks such as changing requirements and limited access to real business data for testing, which the team will manage through short iterations, regular reviews with the supervisor and the use of public retail datasets.

\section{Overall Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

StockSense runs as a sequential pipeline through the modules described in Chapter~6 with each module reading its input from the PostgreSQL database and writing its output to it. The sales are added to the system via built-in pos or via file upload, and they are stored in a single and canonical schema, therefore the rest of the pipeline does not rely on where the sales came from. A demand series is constructed for each product, size, store combination, features are calculated from the history of the series and the forecasting model generates the weekly and monthly forecasts for demand. These predictions are then contrasted with the current stock to yield a redistribution, markdown and reorder recommendation along with an explanation and an estimated financial impact. Forecast and recommendation are done as background jobs and stored in forecast and recommendation tables. Each forecast includes the name of the model used to generate the forecast, allowing for comparison of forecasts and/or replacing the model without having to change the rest of the system. To respect tenant isolation, models are trained separately for each tenant, so one model is shared by all products, sizes and stores of a business but never across businesses.

\section{Data Ingestion}
\label{sec:ingestion}

There are two ways that sales are added to StockSense. The records created from the built-in POS are stored as \texttt{Sale} and \texttt{SaleItem} records with the source marked as POS, and the records created by importing them from CSV or Excel, that were exported from another system, are stored in the same records with the source marked as Upload. All the remaining steps of the pipeline are the same for both sources due to them having the same schema. The pandas library is used to read in the CSV files, and the pandas library and \texttt{openpyxl} are used to read in the Excel files, both in chunks to ensure low memory usage for up to 100,000 rows of data. Once you upload a file, the system displays the columns it has identified, and the owner maps these columns to the canonical ones for product, size, store, date and quantity. Validation and import is a background process, so that the owner can still work even if a large file is being processed. Valid rows are stored in buffer until owner accepts the upload report, and then uploaded in one database transaction which is rolled back entirely if anything is not uploaded successfully, thus failing upload does not leave any data behind.

\section{Data Validation, Cleaning, and Normalization}
\label{sec:validation}

All records submitted are subject to multiple validations before being accepted. The structural checks ensure that all the necessary elements are included and that their data types are correct. The value check includes that the date is valid as well as that the quantity is a number and that the price is not negative. A date, like 03/04/2025, may be interpreted in two different ways, so it is up to the owner to decide which date format to use when the columns are mapped. Referential checks normalize the names of products, sizes and stores by trimming spaces, ignoring case, standardizing sizes (such as converting ``Medium'' to ``M'', etc.), and then check to see if a record corresponds to a variant and a store with the same tenant. If two records are the same, they are identified by a composite key consisting of the store, variant, date, quantity and price; however, they are not deleted because it is possible for two or more identical sales to happen. Negative quantities are accounted for as returns, and extreme quantities are marked as outliers and capped only when training the data – no changes are made to the original data. Rejected and flagged records are reported in the upload report alongside the reason for the rejection/flag, allowing the owner to amend the source record and upload it again.Rejected and flagged records are not saved but are reported in the upload report with the reason for rejection/flag so that the owner can amend the source record and then upload it again.

\section{Demand Series Construction}
\label{sec:series}

After cleaning, the sales are aggregated into weekly and monthly series for each variant and store. Weekly forecasts are made from weekly series and monthly forecasts from monthly series, so that the forecast horizon and the evaluation period always have the same time resolution. The demand of variant $v$ in store $s$ during period $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    y(v,s,t) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}(v,s,t)} q_i,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}(v,s,t)$ is the set of sale items of variant $v$ in store $s$ during period $t$, and $q_i$ is the quantity of sale item $i$. A period with no sales is treated as zero demand only from the date on which the variant first appeared in that store, because before that date the demand is unknown rather than zero.

Series at the size level are often sparse and intermittent, with many periods of zero sales. Each series is thus "smooth", "erratic", "intermittent" or "lumpy" based on the classification created by Syntetos and Boylan that relies on the average inter-demand interval (ADI) and the squared coefficient of variation of the non-zero demands ($\mathrm{CV}^2$), using the generally accepted thresholds of $\mathrm{ADI}=1.32$ and $\mathrm{CV}^2=0.49$. This classification can be used to select appropriate baseline methods, e.g. for intermittent series, Croston's method, and can be used to report the forecast accuracy separately for each type of demand. Another issue is that the actual demand is higher than the sales being recorded when an item is out of stock; as the customers would have not been able to purchase the product. If the stock history is known, then the weeks in which the stock was not available for a majority of the time are then considered censored, and are not included in the training loss, preventing the model from learning stockout periods are periods of low demand. These measures yield a clean series which closely approximates demand, as best as the data permits.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

Each training sample represents one variant of one store at a forecast origin time $t$ and the target is the demand at a later time $t+h$. The features are grouped in a few groups, each representing a set of aspects of demand. Lag features contain the demand for the week that went before the last 0-8 weeks, as well as the same week last year if there is a year's history (which reflects the current level and the annual seasonality). Rolling stats include the average, the standard deviation and the percentage of weeks in the last 4, 8 and 12 weeks that had zero sales, providing information on level, volatility and intermittency. Special events and seasonality are captured in Calendar features, such as the week number, month and holiday/festival flags. The selling price and depth of any discount and the price relative to the category average are price features that capture the effect of pricing on demand. The categorical features include product attributes like category and size, attributes like colour, store and city which enables the global model to share patterns between similar series. Lastly, lifecycle features contain information on how many weeks the first and last sale occurred, representing the product's age. To avoid data leakage, features at time $t$ draw only data that was available prior to $t$; only features known in advance (e.g., calendar events) may reference future time periods.

\section{Demand Forecasting Model}
\label{sec:forecasting}

This section describes the forecasting model, the loss function and forecast horizon, the training and tuning procedure, the statistical baselines, the handling of new tenants and the treatment of forecast uncertainty.

\subsection{Global Gradient-Boosted Model}

The individual series within a specific size level are not long enough or numerous enough to warrant a separate model for each series. Like the global models of Montero-Manso and Hyndman and the approach Bandara et al. took with groups of similar series, StockSense trains one global model per tenant, using all of the tenant's series as training data, and adding product, size and store to the list of categorical features. We chose LightGBM, an implementation of gradient-boosted decision trees. In the M5 competition, gradient-boosted trees performed better than the statistical methods, and LightGBM was able to directly deal with missing data and categorical variables, while training quickly on tabular data, keeping training costs low for a platform serving many tenants. Another benefit is that training a single model per tenant also puts new product variants on the market to immediately benefit from patterns learned from other similar products and stores, which is particularly important for small businesses that often add new variants to the market without any prior sales history.

\subsection{Loss Function and Forecast Horizon}

Demand is never negative and contains many zeros, so squared error is not a suitable loss function. The model therefore uses the Tweedie objective with a power parameter between 1 and 2, which describes a distribution with a point mass at zero and a continuous positive part, matching the shape of sparse sales data. The Poisson objective and squared error are kept as alternatives and will be compared in the experiments. Multi-step forecasts are made with a direct approach, in which a separate model is trained for each step $h = 1,\dots,H$ of the forecast horizon, so that errors do not accumulate as they would with recursive predictions. The forecast for step $h$ is
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}(v,s,t+h) = f_h\big(\mathbf{x}_{v,s,t}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_{v,s,t}$ denotes the features of variant $v$ in store $s$ at time $t$.

\subsection{Training and Tuning}

The hyperparameters of the model, which include the number of leaves, the learning rate, the minimum amount of data per leaf, the feature and bagging fractions and the number of boosting rounds, are tuned with rolling-origin validation. The training window is incrementally grown over time, the model is validated on the time interval after each window and in each fold, early stopping is performed. Random shuffling is never used, as that would give the model a chance of ‘learning' future data during training, and would provide an overly rosy picture of its accuracy. Censored stockout periods are excluded from the loss (see Section $\ref{sec:series}$) using sample weights. Training is done using fixed random seeds to ensure reproducible results, and the optimized parameters are saved along with each trained model, enabling the tracing of each forecast to its respective model and parameters.

\subsection{Statistical Baselines}

Several simple baselines are calculated on the same set of data splits to see if the more complicated model is truly needed. If a year of history is present, the naive forecast is the value at the same time that has been observed in the previous year; otherwise, it is the last observed value. The moving average is a forecast of the average of recent periods while Holt-Winters exponential smoothing, using the \texttt{statsmodels} package, accounts for level, trend and seasonality. The method has been included for intermittent series, as the demand is likely to be zero many times. They are also cost-efficient and simple to explain, and provide a stand-in for tenants who lack sufficient data to use the global model. The simpler method will be chosen for the group of series in these cases if LightGBM does not significantly outperform the best baseline.

\subsection{Initial Training Period}

There is no pre-trained model installed in StockSense; and the data of any tenant is not revealed to any other tenant; hence, a new tenant will have no forecasting model at the beginning. The owner will have to start with entering his/her sales either using the POS or through importing historical sales reports. Till the minimum history becomes available, say, for a few weeks, no forecasting will be available based on any models; and the user will be informed that additional data is required, as stated in UC-10. However, till the availability of the minimum data set, some baseline forecasts may be available using the available data set. As soon as the minimum data is available, the first global model is trained from the data of the tenant itself, and hence forecasting and recommendations based on the model will be available.

\subsection{Forecast Uncertainty}

A quantile forecast in addition to the point forecast is also trained, namely 0.9 quantile, based on the quantile objective function of LightGBM. This forecast is a measure of demand, which will be exceeded only once in ten periods. This forecast is used by the recommendation engine in order to determine a safety stock, and thus, the recommendation engine takes into account not only the expected demand but also the volatility of it. If there is no safety stock, then the recommendation engine will consider the demand above the expected demand as excess and will make recommendations, which will lead to transferring the product from the source store, which will not be able to deal with increased demand. The quantile forecast is also trained with the same features and validation procedure as the point forecast.

\section{Recommendation Engine and Stock Redistribution}
\label{sec:recommendation}

For every variant $v$ and store $s$, the recommendation engine compares the forecast with the stock position over a planning horizon of $H$ weeks. Let $I$ be the stock on hand plus any accepted incoming transfers that have not yet arrived, which are included so that they are not recommended again. Let $D$ be the forecast demand over the next $H$ weeks and $Q_{0.9}$ the upper quantile forecast over the same horizon, so that the safety stock above the expected demand is $SS = \max(0,Q_{0.9}-D)$. The net position of the store is then
\begin{equation}
    N = I - (D + SS).
\end{equation}
If $N > 0$, the store has a surplus of $N$ units, and if $N < 0$, it has a shortage of $-N$ units.

Following the proactive transshipment approach of Paterson et al., transfers are planned before a stockout or dead stock occurs. For each variant, the transfer quantities $x_{ij}$ from a surplus store $i$ to a shortage store $j$ are found by solving a transportation problem,
\begin{align}
    \max_{x}\quad & \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \big(m_j - c_{ij}\big)\, x_{ij} \\
    \text{s.t.}\quad & \sum_{j} x_{ij} \le \text{surplus}_i \quad \forall i, \\
    & \sum_{i} x_{ij} \le \text{shortage}_j \quad \forall j, \\
    & x_{ij} \ge 0,
\end{align}
where $m_j$ is the unit margin at the destination store $j$ and $c_{ij}$ is the unit transfer cost between stores $i$ and $j$. A transfer is recommended only when $m_j - c_{ij} > 0$, that is, when the expected margin of the unit at the destination is larger than the cost of moving it. Transfer quantities must be whole units, and because the constraint matrix of a transportation problem and its supply and demand values are integers, the linear program has an integral optimal solution. Since small retailers have only a few branches, each problem is small and cheap to solve. Transfer lead times and capacity limits are not part of the model at present and are handled as assumptions in the backtest.

If a shortage remains after redistribution, because no other branch has enough surplus, it becomes a reorder recommendation with a suggested order quantity equal to the remaining shortage. Recommendations are always handled in a fixed order of priority, with redistribution first, markdowns second and reorders last, because using existing inventory is usually cheaper and faster than discounting it or buying new stock. This order is the main way in which StockSense differs from tools that consider each store on its own and treat transfers as only one option among many.

%==================================================
\section{Markdown Optimization}
\label{sec:markdown}

Any surplus stock that is not removed by redistribution is passed to the markdown optimizer. Like the method of Caro and Gallien, the optimizer follows a forecast-then-optimize approach, but it is meant to act as an early warning during the season rather than as a clearance process at its end. A variant in a store is flagged as at risk when its weeks of cover are greater than the selling time that remains. When the forecast demand is positive, the weeks of cover are
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{WOC} = \frac{I}{\bar{d}} > T,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{d}$ is the average weekly demand and $T$ is the number of weeks within which the product needs to be sold. If $\bar{d}=0$ and $I>0$, the weeks of cover are undefined, so the item is treated as at risk and handled separately.

For each candidate discount $d$ from a grid, for example from 5\% to 50\% in steps of 5\%, the expected demand is adjusted with a constant price elasticity model,
\begin{equation}
    D(d) = D \cdot (1 - d)^{-\varepsilon}, \qquad
    \text{Sold}(d) = \min\big(I,\; D(d)\big),
\end{equation}
where $D(d)$ is the total adjusted demand over the horizon and $\varepsilon$ is the price elasticity. The system recommends the smallest discount for which the projected sell-through reaches the target, subject to the margin floor $p\,(1-d) \ge c$, where $p$ is the selling price and $c$ is the cost price. A discount is recommended only if it does not lead to a lower expected margin than not discounting at all. Its impact is measured as the difference in margin between the two cases,
\begin{equation}
    \text{Impact} = \text{Sold}(d)\,\big(p(1-d) - c\big) - \text{Sold}(0)\,\big(p - c\big).
\end{equation}
The elasticity $\varepsilon$ is estimated with a log-log regression on the tenant's own price history when the prices of the product have varied enough. Otherwise a cautious estimate for the product category is used, so that the optimizer does not overestimate the effect of a discount for a business with little pricing history.

\section{Explainable Recommendations}
\label{sec:explain}

Each recommendation is stored together with an explanation made of two parts. The first part contains the numbers that describe the situation, which are the forecast demand, the stock level, the net position, the weeks of cover, the source and destination stores, the margins, the transfer costs and the estimated impact of the recommendation. The second part describes the main drivers of the forecast, which are taken from the feature contributions computed by LightGBM through its \texttt{pred\_contrib} option and translated into plain phrases such as ``recent weekly demand'' or ``seasonality''. Both parts are inserted into text templates that produce short sentences such as ``Size M of this item is forecast to sell 12 units at Store B over the next 4 weeks, but only 3 are in stock, while Store A holds 15 units and is forecast to sell only 4''. The explanation is created and saved at the same moment as the recommendation, so it always matches the situation that led to it, even if the stock or the forecast changes later. Because the main part of the explanation comes from the actual quantities used by the decision rule rather than from an approximation of the model, it accurately describes why the recommendation was made, which addresses the concern raised by Rudin about explanations of black box models.

\section{Forecast and Recommendation Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section describes the data, procedures, metrics, and experiments that were used for the forecast evaluation. This section also describes the historical backtest that was used for the recommendation evaluation. All experimental details and result tables are presented in Chapter~9.

\subsection{Data}

Public retail datasets will be utilized to develop and validate the models for the first time. A public dataset like M5 includes sales and prices of goods in individual stores, and hence it is possible to analyze the behavior at the store level and at higher hierarchical levels of the products but there is no data on the sales by size. The behavior at the size level will be analyzed using real sales data of a small retailer if it is possible to obtain the data. Tenants with two or three branches will also be simulated to evaluate the whole pipeline of the system from upload to recommendation. The results obtained from the analysis of the public data will be understood bearing in mind the constraint mentioned in Chapter~2 that the data of a big retailer might not represent the behavior of small retailers.

\subsection{Protocol}

All data splits are chronological such that the models are always trained on older data and tested on newer data, which reflects the way they are supposed to work in real-world situations. Hyperparameters are optimized using rolling origin validation based on an expanding window approach, while the final comparison of models is done on the latest period where none of the models sees any of that period in training or optimization. All models and baselines are evaluated on the same splits, and thus, differences in their performance are because of the models rather than the data. The random seeds are kept fixed such that experiments can be replicated, and the results are reported for both weekly and monthly horizons.

\subsection{Metrics}

The primary accuracy metrics are
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(a_k - f_k\big)^2}, \\
    \mathrm{MAPE} &= \frac{100}{|P|}\sum_{k \in P}\frac{|a_k - f_k|}{a_k}, \qquad P = \{k \mid a_k > 0\},
\end{align}
where $a_k$ is the actual demand and $f_k$ is the forecast.

Series at the size level contain many zeros, and MAPE is undefined when the actual demand is zero. MAPE is therefore computed only on periods with positive actual demand, and the share of periods that had to be excluded is reported alongside it. WAPE, which divides the total absolute error by the total actual sales, and MAE are used as additional measures because they remain valid for sparse series. Results are reported separately for each demand class and for fast-moving and slow-moving items, because an average over all series can hide poor accuracy on exactly the sparse series that matter most for dead stock.

\subsection{Experiments}

There are four experiments that will be conducted to evaluate the key assumptions behind the modeling approach. The first experiment compares the performance of LightGBM to the statistical baseline, thus testing the key hypothesis formulated based on the review of literature in Chapter~3. The second experiment compares the global model and individual models per each series, to see if the shared information helps in improving the forecasting accuracy for limited history series. The third experiment evaluates the forecasting accuracy depending on the history size of the training data (for example, 4, 8 and 12 weeks), to understand how many data a new tenant should submit to obtain reliable forecasts, thus setting the minimum history requirement for the initial training period. The last experiment compares Tweedie/Poisson objectives to squared error to validate the selection of the loss function for the case of sparse and non-negative demand.

\subsection{Recommendation Backtest}

The value of recommendations is measured through replaying previous sales. In each cut-off date, the engine calculates the recommendations based only on the available information before that cut-off date. The recommendations for transfers and markdowns are then implemented on a simulated stock, where the subsequent weeks are replayed using the real sales as demand. The simulation considers the transfer lead-time, when the stock is considered to be out-of-stock in both locations during the transfer process, and the markdown is accepted from the date of effectiveness of the markdown. The transfer does not affect the demand, but the markdown affects the demand using the elasticities approach. The comparison is done with another run without taking any action, where the number of dead-stock units whose cover exceeds T weeks, units of lost sales due to stock-out and the sell-through rate are considered.

\section{Iterative Development}
\label{sec:iterative}

StockSense is developed iteratively in increments, and each iteration is concluded with testing the system, described in Chapter~7, and the supervisor's review. The first increment develops the multi-tenant architecture, which includes tenancy, users, products, inventory, POS, as well as ingestion and validation pipeline. The second increment conducts the offline forecasting experiments in Jupyter Notebook in order to develop and compare features, baselines, and LightGBM model on the historical data. The third increment integrates the selected model into the system as a batch forecasting job and adds the recommendation engine and redistribution algorithm to the system. The fourth increment integrates markdown optimization, explanations, notifications, and the forecast and recommendation dashboard views into the system. First developing forecasting models offline allows selecting the best model on the basis of the measurement results prior to integrating it into the system and, therefore, allows to test the entire platform end to end using simple baselines while developing the models.

The method by which StockSense converts each sale into a replenishment decision was explained in this chapter. \section{System Overview}

StockSense is a multi-tenant SaaS application that assists small and medium-sized retailers in reducing dead stock and stockouts using sales data provided by them. Each organization is a separate tenant with its users, stores, products, inventories, and sales. Sales are entered either manually using the POS built into the system or through import from uploaded CSV and Excel files. The application makes demand predictions for the product, size, and store levels and gives actions that should be taken along with their explanations on one web-based dashboard. StockSense is organized into modules that are listed below in the subsections. Each of these modules has one specific task, and they communicate via the database and clearly defined interfaces.

\subsection{Tenant and User Management}

This module is responsible for the registration of companies, the login of the user and their role. Once the company gets registered, this module generates a tenant, a free subscription and an owner account all at once. Then it ensures that the owner, the store manager or the administrator can have access only to the data associated with his/her own tenant and the role he/she holds. Every request to the backend has the tenant id and the role of the user in it, which this module verifies before accessing or modifying the data. This module is also responsible for the invitations of the store managers by the owner and the suspending and activation of the tenants by the administrator so that no user belonging to a suspended tenant can log in.

\subsection{POS and Inventory Management}

This module comprises the free portion of StockSense and serves as the primary means by which sales are entered into the system. This module tracks all sales and maintains the current stock of each item by size and store. Both the sale itself and the subsequent reduction in stock are tracked in one single database transaction, ensuring that the inventory is always in sync with sales. The module also allows for manual corrections following a physical count, but such manual corrections require a reason and are recorded, along with the completion of all stock transfer transactions generated from an accepted redistribution suggestion. Since this module is used by the store managers during regular business hours, speed and availability are crucial features.

\subsection{Data Ingestion and Cleaning}

In this module, the historical sales data are imported from the CSV or Excel files, verified, and then invalid records are detected, and the data are converted into the standardized format which is used in the system. The module helps companies that already operate on another POS system or spreadsheet to integrate into StockSense system without making changes in their current system, and it also helps them have their forecasting model earlier since their historical data are available from Day One. The module displays the detected columns so that the owner can map them into the required columns and select the date format, performs the tests specified in Chapter~5 behind the scenes, and displays the upload report with accepted and rejected rows before any import takes place.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting}

This module provides the forecasts of demand for the weekly and monthly period for each product-size-store combination. It learns a global LightGBM model on the basis of the sales data provided by each tenant, along with their statistical counterparts that serve as a baseline for comparison, and stores the forecasted values under the title of the model used for forecasting. New tenants have to collect sufficient amount of sales data in order to start displaying model-based forecasts and during this period, the module will notify that more data is required. This module works as an asynchronous scheduled job so that no performance bottlenecks will occur for the rest of the system. In case of failed forecasting, previous forecasts are stored for proper functioning of the dashboard and recommendations.

\subsection{Stock Redistribution and Reorder Recommendations}

In this module, the forecast demand is compared to the existing stock levels of the branch and determines whether there is a surplus or a shortage of each variant of product taking safety stock calculated from the forecast upper quantile into consideration. The module starts with the recommendation of transferring stocks between the branches where there is a surplus and branches that have shortages by solving a small transport problem that aims to maximize the expected profit margin in case of transfer cost. Only in case when surplus from other branches is insufficient to compensate the shortage, then the reorder of the rest amount is recommended.

\subsection{Markdown Optimization}

The present module is designed to recognize slow-moving stock and advises on discounting it well ahead of time such that it sells and is not classified as dead stock. The module runs on the excess after re-allocation, recognizes those products which have weeks of cover higher than the time left for sale, and analyzes a range of discounts through a price elasticity framework. The minimum discount required to meet the sell-through objective without lowering the price below the cost price and lowering the projected margin is recommended. Products with no forecast demand but with stock on hand are recognized separately. As for all other recommendations, each discount recommendation also comes with reasoning and estimated impact on margin.

\subsection{Explainable Recommendations}

Each recommendation generated by StockSense is presented along with an explanation for why the recommendation has been made and its estimated financial impact so that the owner understands and trusts the recommendation prior to acting on it. The explanation is derived from two different types of information; namely, the quantities used in making the decision, such as forecast demand, inventory, transportation cost and margin, and the factors driving the forecast, which are extracted from the feature contributions of the LightGBM model. These two types of information are translated into natural language using text templates. The explanation is recorded along with the recommendation so that it always explains the conditions under which the recommendation has been made.

\subsection{Dashboard and Reporting}

This module gives a simple and accessible web interface that includes sales, stock, forecasts, and recommendations for the owners and managers of stores. The owners will have access to information related to all the branches, whereas the managers will be provided with information related to their store only. The dashboard consists of the monthly sales, items that are close to being out of stock or dead stock, recent sales and the status of stock of each branch, and in case of subscription, forecast numbers, recommendations list, along with past sales numbers and option to approve and ignore recommendations. The free tenants will have an upgrade notice instead of paid portions. As forecasts and recommendations are calculated and stored, the dashboard just needs to read them from there.

\section{Design Considerations}

This section lists the assumptions, dependencies, constraints and goals that affect the design of StockSense, so that they are understood and handled before implementation starts.

\subsection{Assumptions and Dependencies}

The design depends on assumptions about the environment of the users, their skills and the possibility that the requirements will change during development.

\subsubsection{Operating System and Browser}

As StockSense is a web application, it does not require any specific OS on the side of the user. The latter requires having an updated browser such as Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari running on a laptop, desktop or tablet computer. The server-side requirements include having the Linux OS with Python, PostgreSQL and libraries for forecasting as specified in Chapter~4 along with a hosting company capable of providing sufficient computing resources for background processes. Limiting the choice of the browser to those currently used makes it possible to employ modern web technology on the interface side, while the architecture of the server allows offloading all computationally heavy tasks from the user computer and makes it possible for even an old computer to handle the POS and dashboard without issues.

\subsubsection{End User Characteristics}

This design requires the user to have a reliable internet connection because both POS and dashboard operate inside the browser and save their data to the server. The owner and the manager are expected to have knowledge of using computers for routine tasks but no special technical knowledge or skills in analytics. This is the foundation for most parts of the design, including clear language descriptions, hiding statistics-related terminology, and easy processes of logging sales and uploading files. The other assumption is that the retailer has either historical data on its sales, obtained from the existing POS system or spreadsheet, or is ready to enter sales data into the integrated POS until there is enough data for forecasting.

\subsubsection{Possible Changes in Functionality}

The accuracy of predictions will be affected by the quality and quantity of sales data, which cannot be assessed prior to the testing of models. The functionality of the system might thus undergo modification based on the results of experiments conducted using the datasets as well as the recommendations of the advisor. For instance, the forecasting algorithm can be changed in some cases when a certain baseline proves to be more accurate, the minimal history needed for new customers can be modified, and the recommendation rules might have to be changed after the backtest. It is hoped that the modular structure will allow implementing such changes without altering the other components of the system, since modules are connected through the shared data and interfaces only.

\subsection{General Constraints}

The design must also respect several constraints that come from the environment in which StockSense will run, the data it receives and the security and performance it must provide.

\subsubsection{Hardware and Software Environment}

Tasks like forecasting and data processing require far more computational power than regular requests for pages. For this reason, this design has been divided into separate background tasks that can run independent of the website itself. The interface needs to function across various devices and browsers, hence a responsive design and thorough testing across screen sizes is required. The use of third-party libraries like LightGBM, statsmodels, pandas and openpyxl also puts certain restrictions on the development process. This includes restrictions on the types of files that can be handled, and Python versions that can be used. Keeping the libraries up to date is also important, as they require security patches and performance upgrades from time to time. Background tasks will also need to be monitored, as a failed job would mean the forecasts remain unchanged for the tenant.

\subsubsection{End-User Environment}

Some shops might experience slow or unstable internet connection, which means that the POS has to ensure that its requests are short and that the user can see if the sale is successfully stored. Long operations such as uploading and forecasting should be performed on the server continuously despite connection interruptions and then be reported back by the user. The fact that many store owners and managers lack knowledge of data analysis means that all forecasts and recommendations presented to them must be expressed in business terms and not include any technical terminology. These limitations influence the network architecture and the choice of words on each and every page used in the software. Thus, the interface design takes into account the worst possible situation for it to be effective even in poorly equipped shops with poor internet connection.

\subsubsection{Data Format Differences}

Files from various enterprises have different column names, date formats, and product names. In one case, a column name is ``Qty'', while in the other, it is ``Quantity''; in one case, the dates are formatted in a day first format while in the other, dates are formatted in the month first format; sizes may be represented as ``Medium'', ``Med'', or ``M''. All these must be normalized to one format before being used in the forecast process. This is achieved through the column mapping phase where an appropriate date format is selected by the owner together with product, size, and store name normalization rules and all rows which cannot be matched are reported back to the owner. This helps make the forecast process independent of the multiple formats of the external systems.

\subsubsection{Data Repository and Tenant Isolation}

No business should ever be able to view the data from any other business, and this is the main restriction on the data repository. All the records owned by any particular business thus belong to a tenant, and all queries are limited to the tenant of the user making the query. The system must be scalable as more and more tenants get added and as each tenant accumulates more sales history, while retaining good performance. This can be achieved through indexing of tenant, store and variant IDs that are present in all the queries, and caching the forecast values so that they do not need to be recomputed every time. The isolation is ensured on the back end as well, and not just in the interface, meaning that a user can never access data of another tenant even by making queries to the API directly. Backups and logs must be subject to the same restriction.

\subsubsection{Security Requirements}

Each individual accesses the system with his or her own account, and he or she is responsible for maintaining the privacy of the password, and passwords are stored in the system only in the form of hashes. The access to the data will be determined by the role of the user, and that is why the store manager will access only his or her particular store, and the system administrator will be able to manage the tenants but not having access to the sales information of them. The data should be safe both during transfer and storage using encryption and backup procedures. In addition, it is necessary to follow the secure programming guidelines such as the usage of parameterized queries and keeping secret keys out of the source code.

\subsubsection{Performance Requirements}

Prediction and large upload processing have to happen in the background such that they do not get in the way of POS and the dashboard in any way. Frequently required information such as current predictions and open recommendations is cached and served immediately rather than recalculated with every query. Invalid inputs should be identified quickly through meaningful error messages rather than long processing times ending up in errors. More concrete performance requirements like the saving of a POS sale in less than two seconds and processing of a CSV file with 100,000 rows in less than five minutes can be found in Chapter~4 and are tested in the non-functional test cases of Chapter~7. Long listings such as those in the inventory page will be fetched in pages rather than at once. This keeps the application responsive even when the database of a tenant increases in size over time.

\subsubsection{Interoperability Requirements}

The StockSense system will need to be able to accept CSV and Excel files exported from external point-of-sale systems and spreadsheet applications since this is the method used by existing businesses to incorporate their history into the system. The modules need to have documented interfaces within the system in order for them to be able to develop and test independently and also incorporate other data sources like direct connections to popular point-of-sale systems in the future without having to modify the forecasting and recommendations modules. This is the reason why the API for the web interface has a consistent design. The consistency of the interfaces also makes it possible for the web dashboard and other future mobile applications to utilize the same API without duplicating efforts.

\subsubsection{Verification and Validation Requirements}

The upload process needs to be validated on files that have faults or damages to ensure that any bad data is filtered out without damaging existing data. Tenant isolation needs to be validated for any potential data leakage between companies or stores within a company. The forecasts need to be validated using historical data that is unseen by the model based on the procedure discussed in Chapter~5, and the recommendations need to be validated using the historical backtest. All the functional requirements will be associated with at least one test case such that the validation of the system will trace back to the functional requirements. This verification effort will be planned since the beginning of development rather than waiting until the end of development because it will allow the finding of defects when it is inexpensive to fix. The outcomes will be documented in Chapter~7 and Chapter~9 of FYP-2.

\subsection{Goals and Guidelines}

The design follows four goals that guide decisions whenever several options are possible.

\subsubsection{The KISS Principle}

The design must remain as simple as possible, following the principle of keeping things simple, so that the system is easy to maintain for developers and easy to use for non-technical users. Wherever a simple method gives similar results to a complex one, the simple method is preferred, which is why the redistribution problem is solved as a small linear program and the markdown optimizer uses a simple elasticity model. Simplicity also makes the recommendations easier to explain, since a rule that can be stated in one sentence is much easier for an owner to trust than the output of a complex procedure. Simple designs are also easier to test thoroughly, which matters for a small team with limited time.

\subsubsection{Separation of Tenant Data}

Each business should have its secure place for data, which means this goal has higher priority than convenience or efficiency. Each model should be trained per tenant, all data should have tenant ID, and each request is verified using this tenant ID. Even those features which would be more efficient with sharing data like benchmarking per tenants, should use only aggregated and anonymous data and this can happen only in case when there are enough tenants, so no individual company could be recognized. This goal is based on the assumption that small companies will share only their sales data with the platform which they can trust. Each feature which can compromise isolation is refused despite possible benefits in improving forecast accuracy.

\subsubsection{Modularity and Extensibility}

Forecasting, recommendations, POS, data ingestion, and markdown optimization have to be separated in order to allow, for instance, adding a new forecasting algorithm without affecting other components of the system. The input and output interfaces of each module are connected to the database, and forecasts include information about the model that created them. Such a modular approach allows the addition and testing of multiple models and recommendation rules at once. Also, it makes it possible for the team to test each module separately. The modular architecture limits the impact of failures since failure in one module, such as an incorrect forecast, will not affect the operation of others. In such a way, the maintainability and reliability of the system can be achieved using modularity. These quality attributes are described in Chapter~4.

\subsubsection{Explainability}

Every recommendation needs the justification for it and the cost estimation. This objective influences both the development of recommendation system, that maintains all the figures required to make each decision, and the design of database that maintains every explanation along with corresponding recommendation. This objective also influences the method selection, since preference is given to those decision rules that can be stated in plain English, whereas the methods which yield results that are not explainable to a business owner are not used. Explainability is considered a mandatory part of every new feature and not an addition to it since unexplainable recommendations will not be used by a user. This is the reason why every explanation appears along with recommendation on the dashboard, and not in some separate window.

\subsection{Development Methods}

The StockSense system will be developed in iterations. The first iteration will include the tenant management, product management, inventory management modules, the POS and data intake, which will be combined and represent the free tier that provides data for future modules. The second iteration will include the forecasting module, recommendation engine, markdown optimizer and the dashboards that visualize them, starting from development of the forecasting model offline and later integrating into the platform. Each iteration includes several sprints that result in a working version of the system, tests, and a review meeting with the supervisor. An iteration is reviewed before the next iteration begins, in order to be able to fix issues and adjust the plan according to the experimental results.

\section{System Architecture}

StockSense uses a hierarchical web architecture illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}. Users interact with the web dashboard and POS application which make requests to the backend through the API. The API identifies the user and the tenant and routes their requests to the correct module. Cleaned sales data received through the ingestion module and the POS application are fed into the forecasting module. The forecast data along with the present stock from the POS and inventory modules are then fed into the recommendation module. Surplus stock not able to be allocated elsewhere is sent to the markdown optimizer. All the data is stored in the database and returned to the user through the dashboard. Long-running tasks like training, forecasting, and large uploads are handled asynchronously in the background and do not hinder the interactive components of the system.

\subsection{Subsystem Architecture}

The architecture consists of six subsystems. User and tenant management subsystem contains the businesses and their users, processes the registration, logins and roles and makes sure that any user has access to their tenant only. POS and inventory subsystem keeps the current stock of all items, sizes and stores and logs all sales. Data ingestion subsystem reads the incoming CSV or Excel files, validates them, marks the invalid entries and transforms the files into the standard internal format. Forecasting subsystem analyses the sales history of every tenant and performs forecasting of weekly and monthly demand for the items, sizes and stores separately, trains the model on the tenant's own data and does not generate any forecasts until there is enough history. Recommendation subsystem compares the forecasts with the stock of each branch, suggests transferring the stocks between branches before making new orders and keeps every recommendation together with its justification and expected effect. Finally, markdown optimization subsystem detects the slow-moving items and suggests to reduce the price before they stop selling. Subsystems share the database but do not depend on each other's internal logic.

\section{Architectural Strategies}

The architectural strategies below describe the main technical decisions that implement the design goals.

\subsection{Multi-Tenant Architecture}\label{sec:multitenant}

Many businesses use the same database; however, each organization’s data is logically separated from the others. All critical records have a tenant identifier, and all queries are evaluated by the tenant of the logged-in user before any reading or writing is done. Shared database architecture allows keeping cost per tenant low – this is important for a platform that provides a free plan for small organizations, while ensuring the impossibility of viewing other organizations’ data. Predictive models are also trained per tenant, hence isolation is provided not only for the data but also for the model. It becomes simple to add new tenants, and all checks related to tenants are done within one layer of the backend and thus cannot be missed in modules.

\subsection{Modular Architecture}

The system consists of the modules of forecasting, recommendations, POS, ingestion, and markdown optimization. Every module performs its task and interacts with other modules via the stored data and interfaces. In case any module changes – for instance, a new forecasting module becomes available – the rest does not have to be changed very much. This facilitates the testing process because every module can be tested separately using some predefined test data and helps the developers work simultaneously on different modules. This modular architecture is a consequence of the maintainability and flexibility qualities described in Chapter~4. The interfaces between the modules are kept small and are well documented. It gives an idea about the input data of every module and output data of every module, reducing the chances of misunderstanding between the developers.

\subsection{Programming Languages and Frameworks}

The frontend will be created using React because it enables the creation of a responsive one-page interface for the dashboard and POS. The backend Representational State Transfer (REST) API will be written using Python with FastAPI, which is compatible with the Python data science packages for the forecasting algorithm. The LightGBM will be used as the main forecasting algorithm, the statistical models will be provided by statsmodels, and the pandas with openpyxl will allow processing CSV and Excel files. The use of the same language for the REST API and the forecasting algorithm means that the entire backend uses only one language, making it easier to integrate the algorithms into the platform after conducting the offline experiments. All the tools are open-source and commonly used software, which means that the cost of the platform will remain low and it is easy to find the documentation and support. It will also be possible to apply the skills of the team to minimize the time for learning the new tools.

\subsection{Database}

All business data will be stored in PostgreSQL, a relational database. Every table storing business data includes a tenant ID, which enables the tenant isolation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:multitenant}. The use of the relational database is justified by the high degree of structure and interconnectedness of the data in StockSense, where all sales, inventories, forecasts and recommendations are related to variants, stores and tenants, and transactions are necessary to ensure consistency of sales and inventory. The database design is described in detail in the database design chapter of Chapter~4. The most frequently used queries will be fast due to the indexing of the tenant, store and variant IDs. PostgreSQL is open-source and mature, as well as being actively supported by hosting companies, which will make the cost of operating the platform low.

\subsection{Background Processing}

The operations of forecasting and cleaning of big files will be quite time-consuming. They will be performed as background processes and the user will be alerted when their results are ready. Thus, the dashboard and the POS will stay fast. There will also be background processes for retraining of the models, as well as refreshing of forecasts and recommendations on a regular basis along with the arrival of new sales data. Performing these tasks apart from the HTTP request ensures that any demanding process of one tenant will not affect the rest of the tenants’ processes, while also making it possible to give more computational power to the background tasks when more tenants appear. The status of each background process will be recorded, which will allow users to know the state of the process: running, completed or failed.

\subsection{Error Detection and Recovery}

The system will record unsuccessful attempts at uploading files, rejected records, and unsuccessful runs for forecasts with sufficient details that will enable the user to determine the cause. Uploading is carried out within one transaction and in case of failure, it is undone so that the existing data is never left partially complete. In the event that the process of running forecasts is unsuccessful, then the previously created forecasts are retained on the dashboard and are never deleted so that the user can continue working with the forecast until the issue is sorted out and a successful run is done. In case the job runs unsuccessfully several times, the system automatically retries the job and notifies of the repeated failure so that it can be analyzed. Logs do not contain sensitive business information and passwords.

\section{Domain Model/Class Diagram}

The domain model of StockSense is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:class-diagram}. As a result, it is based on the data dictionary from Chap.~4, where any class belonging to a business has only one owner, i.e., the business, and foreign keys of the database are represented by the associations between classes. The Tenant is the class representing a business on the platform and having one or several Subscriptions that describe the history of free and paid plans of the business. The Tenant possesses many Stores, Products, and UploadJobs and has many Users, the user's role defining whether a User is a Business Owner, a Store Manager, or a System Administrator, where the System Administrator is not associated with any Tenant. A Store Manager manages one Store, and a Store can have several Users. A Product has one or more ProductVariants such as sizes and has its own SKU, and an Inventory record relates one ProductVariant to one Store with some quantity. A Sale happens in one Store and includes one or more SaleItems referring to one ProductVariant, and Sale may be associated with an UploadJob or with nothing for POS sales.

From the analytical point of view, a Forecast contains the forecasted demand of a single ProductVariant at a single Store during a week or month period. The Recommendation is an abstract entity with three subclasses: RedistributionRecommendation, ReorderRecommendation and MarkdownRecommendation. Each recommendation is related to one ProductVariant, it relates to a Store, it can be calculated using a Forecast and it is accepted or rejected by a User. A RedistributionRecommendation is also related to a destination Store, and when it is accepted, it creates a StockTransfer that is a record of transferring stocks between two Stores. Finally, a Notification is received by a single User, and it can be related to a Recommendation. For example, to inform a store manager about approval of a transfer that involves the store.

\section{Policies and Tactics}

The policies and tactics below describe how StockSense will be built, tested and maintained, and which practices the team will follow during implementation.

\subsection{Tools to be Used}

Various software frameworks will be used in the StockSense project. The web dashboard and the POS screens will be built using React framework, and the backend REST API will be written in Python using FastAPI or Django and it will take care of authentication, the tenant check, and the communication between modules. All the business data will be stored in PostgreSQL, where there will be a unique identifier for each tenant in all the tables that store the data of one particular business. The CSV and Excel files uploaded by the users will be preprocessed and cleaned using pandas and openpyxl libraries. Data preprocessing, the grouping of the similar time series, and the calculation of the error metrics will be done using scikit-learn and NumPy libraries, the main prediction algorithm will be LightGBM and the baseline statistics will be calculated using the statsmodels library. The experiments with various algorithms will be performed in Jupyter Notebook before one will be chosen and incorporated in the platform.

\subsection{Coding Guidelines and Conventions}

Both the backend and the forecasting code will be developed using Python programming language, while the frontend will use JavaScript with React framework. The team will apply agreed naming standards in both languages, create short and readable functions, and document places where the logic is not straightforward, such as forecasting and redistribution problem formulation. In order to clearly separate responsibilities and find the code quickly, each module should be placed in its own package. All changes will go through the review process by another team member prior to being committed to the main branch. Secure coding techniques will be applied consistently, for example, through the password hashing, usage of parameterized database queries, and absence of any secret keys in the source code. The above coding practices provide consistency of the code among all three team members.

\subsection{User Interface}

The StockSense system is aimed at business owners and shop managers, not tech-savvy people. The program is supposed to be easy to understand and operate for the users without any special education or training. It is expected to be intuitive to make the first-time user enter their first sale or follow the first recommendation without referring to the manual. Forecasts will be displayed in the form of charts right next to the sales, and every recommendation will be provided with explanations that are clear for everybody. All the error messages will be expressed in simple and understandable language, explaining what the problem is and how to solve it. For instance, when there is something wrong with the uploaded file, the message will indicate what rows or columns are causing the problem. All the interface screens will have the same colour scheme and layout so that once the user gets acquainted with one of them, he/she will know how to use other screens as well.

\subsection{Extensibility}

The application will be constructed in a modular fashion where each component will have a specific responsibility, including POS and inventory, data acquisition, forecasting, recommendations, and markdown optimizations. Since each component is distinct from the other components, it will be possible to modify a single component without impacting the others. This will be especially useful when designing the forecasting engine, which will be flexible enough to allow multiple models to be tested side-by-side without impacting other parts of the application because each forecast contains information on the model used. The forecasting engine is a general-purpose component, and it can be used by any business which has variant products, product sizes, or product locations. Similarly, the recommendation engine has been built in a fashion which allows new rules to be introduced into the application. Future expansion such as POS integration, seasonality and promotional offers, and businesses without historical sales figures will not have much effect on the application code.

\subsection{Tenant Data Isolation and Security}

All records in the system will be associated with a tenant, and the backend will check whether a tenant owns the records before processing any request to read or update them. Availability of features will depend on the role of a user, so a store manager will be able to see and modify only the data related to the store assigned to him/her. All communication will be done via HTTPS protocol. The system administrator will be able to manage tenants and plans, but he/she won’t have access to sales data unless this is needed for support. Passwords will be stored using only secure hashes, and the session will expire if it remains idle for some time. The isolation between tenants and the restriction on accessing data based on roles will be checked explicitly using special tests that will try to access the data from other tenants or other stores, and such access will be forbidden.

\subsection{Data Validation}

The uploaded sales data will first be validated prior to its submission into the forecasting pipeline. In case of invalid or partial data records, either they will be marked for errors or simply discarded. An upload report will notify the owner about both the accepted and rejected data along with the respective reasons why some of the records were rejected. Only then, once the report is verified and approved by the owner, will the data be imported in one transaction so that any failed upload process would not harm any already saved data. Data will also be validated for entries made via the POS, like for instance, entering negative quantities or sales of products that are non-existent. As the forecasting results depend entirely on the quality of the data, it is considered as an integral part of the system.

\subsection{Explainable Recommendations}

Every time a recommendation is generated, all the reasons for it, including the forecast demand, the existing stock, the excess/deficit and the originating branch, will be saved along with the financial impact estimated. This way, the owner of the company will be able to see the reasons for generating a particular recommendation and will be able to choose whether to act upon it or not. It is important to note that the reasons for the recommendation are saved immediately after generating it in order to save the exact situation that led to generating that particular recommendation. Recommendations are saved with their current status which can either be pending, actioned or dismissed.

\subsection{System Testing}

Testing approach of StockSense will include unit testing, integration testing, and end-to-end testing. Unit tests will be used to validate individual parts of the system, e.g., the validation rules and update of the inventory after the sale is made. Integration tests will validate the interrelation of API, the database, and modules as well as testing of REST endpoints using Postman. End-to-end tests will include full user scenarios: registration of the business, logging in, sale recording, file upload, and making decisions based on the recommendations. Project-specific tests will ensure that no data leaks between tenants occur, that role-based access works for all types of users, and that upload pipeline handles partially filled and poorly formatted files correctly. Forecasting models will be validated against the statistical baselines on a test dataset, which none of the forecasting models were trained on. Before release, the performance goals established in the requirements chapter will be validated, such as saving POS sale in less than 2 seconds and file of 100,000 records in less than 5 minutes.

\subsection{Software Maintenance}

The source code will be stored in a version control GitHub repository and tracking of bugs and tasks will be done there so as not to miss any bug. The failed upload, rejected entries, and forecast errors will be recorded, thus making it easy to debug. With time as the sales trends change, forecast accuracy will reduce if the model is not updated; hence, the models will be regularly updated to increase the accuracy of the forecasts. The database will be regularly backed up to ensure that the business data can be recovered in case the database fails. Dependancies such as Python libraries and the front-end dependancies will be updated regularly to receive security updates. This ensures reliability and accuracy of the system after deployment.

\subsection{Algorithms}

The algorithms to be implemented in StockSense will be selected based on their efficiency in terms of time and space requirements and will be analyzed with respect to Big-O notation. LightGBM, which is an ensemble of gradient-boosted trees, has been highly efficient on large retail datasets and has shown strong performance in the M5 competition. A separate model will be created for each tenant on all of its product types and sizes, and new tenants should have enough history of sales data to enable forecasting. Baseline statistical models from the statsmodels library will be retained to test whether the complicated model is required at all. The recommendation algorithm compares forecasted demand against current stock of all branches for every variant, pairs branches with excess stock against branches with shortages, considers the leftover stock in excess for potential markdowns and proposes reordering of the stock. The markdown algorithm issues an alert when the sales ratio of the product indicates that it will not be sold within its selling period so that appropriate actions can be taken well in advance. As a business normally has only a few branches, pairing them in this way is computationally inexpensive. Database tables will be indexed on their primary keys and tenant identifiers, the latest forecasts will be cached instead of recomputed, and uploads and model training will run in the background.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter described the high and low-level designs of StockSense. The architectural proposal suggests having modules responsible for managing tenants, POS and inventory, ingesting data, performing forecasts, generating recommendations, optimizing markdown and reporting through dashboards which all interact through the database and its interface. Tenant isolation, role-based access control, data validation, background processing and database indexing are the fundamentals of the system security and scalability. The design provides support for the main decision flow of the project where the demand is forecasted first, redistribution of the available inventory is considered next, markdowns are recommended for the slow moving inventory and new orders are made only when needed. Domain model and sequence diagrams illustrate how every use case is implemented in the system layers while the policies and tactics illustrate how the system is going to be developed, tested and maintained. Testing and maintenance policy is going to be applied to ensure that the system works correctly, efficiently, securely and accurately both before and after the implementation.

\subsection{Development Environment}

The backend will be written in Python with FastAPI, and the web dashboard and the POS screens will be written in React. All data will be stored in PostgreSQL, and every business table will have a tenant identifier. The team will use Visual Studio Code as the editor, Git and GitHub for version control, Postman for testing the API and Jupyter Notebook for the forecasting experiments. Each team member will run the full system locally, with the backend, the frontend and a PostgreSQL database, so that changes can be tested before they are merged. The team will work in short iterations, and the supervisor will review the results at the end of each iteration. Code will be merged into the main branch only after review by another team member, which keeps the main branch in a working state and spreads knowledge of each module across the team.

\subsection{Tenant and User Management}

This module will handle business registration, login and the management of roles. When a business signs up, a tenant, a free subscription and an owner account will be created in a single database transaction, so that a failure part of the way through cannot leave an incomplete tenant behind. Only password hashes will be stored in the database. After login, every request will carry the tenant identifier and the role of the user, and the backend will check both on every read and write of business data, so that no request can reach data that belongs to another tenant or, for store managers, to another store. The module will also let the owner invite store managers by email and assign them to a store, and it will let the system administrator suspend and reactivate tenants, with the rule that users of a suspended tenant cannot log in until the tenant is reactivated.

\subsection{Store, Product and Inventory Management}

The owner will be able to create, modify and archive stores, define products together with their sizes and variants, and invite store managers. Each variant will have an SKU that is unique within the tenant, and an attempt to create a duplicate SKU will be rejected with a clear message. A store that still has inventory or sales history will be archived instead of deleted, so that its history remains available for forecasting. Inventory will be stored for each product variant in each store, and a manual correction of the inventory, for example after a physical count, will require a reason that is logged together with the change. A store manager will be able to view and change only the data of the assigned store. Together these functions provide the catalog and stock data on which the POS, the forecasting module and the recommendation engine depend.

\subsection{POS Module}

On the POS screen, a store manager will select the product and the size, enter the quantity and complete the sale. The system will first check whether enough stock is available and will warn the manager before confirming a sale that would exceed it. Once the sale is confirmed, the sale, the sold items and the updated stock levels will be stored in one transaction, so that the inventory always matches the recorded sales. The goal is to complete this within two seconds, as required in Chapter~4, because the POS is used while customers wait at the counter. The POS will remain available even when the forecasting or upload modules are busy, since it does not depend on them. As the free part of the platform, the POS is also the main source of the sales data that later feeds the forecasting and recommendation modules.

\subsection{Data Ingestion and Cleaning}

This module will accept CSV and Excel files. CSV files will be parsed with pandas in chunks, while Excel files will be parsed with pandas and openpyxl in batches, so that memory use stays low for files of up to 100,000 rows. The owner will match the detected columns to the standard fields of product, size, store, date and quantity and will choose the date format. The system will then validate the structure, values and references of every row, normalize the names of products and sizes, for example by turning ``Medium'' into ``M'', detect returns and unusual values, and mark possible duplicates without removing them. The upload report will show the accepted and rejected rows together with the reason for each rejection. Only after the owner approves the report will the rows be imported, in one transaction that is rolled back if any part of it fails.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting}

Forecasting will first be developed and tested offline in Jupyter Notebook. The steps are to build weekly and monthly demand series for every variant and store, to compute the lag, rolling, calendar, price and lifecycle features, to train one global LightGBM model per tenant with the Tweedie objective, and to compare its performance with the naive, seasonal naive, moving average, Holt-Winters and Croston baselines. An upper quantile model will also be trained to derive the safety stock used by the recommendation engine. The hyperparameters will be tuned with rolling-origin validation and without any shuffling of the data. The selected model will then be integrated into a background job that forecasts demand and writes the results, together with the model name, into the forecast table. For a new tenant without enough history, no model forecast will be produced, and the user will be asked to record or upload more data.

\subsection{Recommendation Engine}

For each variant and store, the engine will compare the stock on hand with the forecast demand and the safety stock in order to detect a surplus or a shortage. The surplus stock will then be allocated to the stores with shortages by solving a small transportation problem, and a transfer will be suggested only if the margin at the destination is greater than the cost of the transfer. If a shortage still remains after redistribution, a reorder recommendation will be generated for the remaining quantity. Accepted transfers that have not yet arrived will be counted as incoming stock, so the same transfer is never recommended twice. The engine will be implemented in Python, with SciPy used to solve the transportation problems, and it will run as a background job after each forecasting run so that the recommendations always reflect the latest forecasts.

\subsection{Markdown Optimizer}

The stock that remains in surplus after redistribution will be evaluated by its weeks of cover. If an item is not expected to sell within the time left, the optimizer will try discounts of 5\%, 10\% and so on up to 50\%, adjusting the expected demand with the price elasticity of the product. The smallest discount that reaches the sell-through target without going below the cost price will be chosen, provided that it does not reduce the expected margin compared with not discounting at all. The estimated change in profit will be stored as the impact of the recommendation. Elasticity will be estimated from the tenant's own price history when it is varied enough, and otherwise a cautious estimate for the category will be used, so that the optimizer does not suggest discounts that are deeper than necessary.

\subsection{Explainable Recommendations}

All recommendations will be stored together with the figures that led to them, which are the forecast demand, the stock, the surplus or shortage, the stores involved, the margin, the cost and the impact, and with the main forecast drivers obtained from the LightGBM feature contributions. These values will be inserted into text templates, so that the owner reads a short and simple sentence rather than a score or a table of numbers. Because the explanation is created at the same moment as the recommendation and stored with it, it will always describe the situation that produced the recommendation, even if the stock or the forecast changes later. This part of the system is central to the goal of making ML-based advice understandable and trustworthy for owners who have no background in data analysis.

\subsection{Dashboard, Alerts and Subscription}

The dashboard will show the monthly sales, the items at risk of stockout and dead stock, the recent sales, the status of each branch and the open recommendations. The owner or a store manager will be able to approve or reject a recommendation, and an approved redistribution will create a stock transfer and notify both stores. On the free plan, the forecast and recommendation sections will be replaced by an upgrade notice. The subscription component will switch the tenant between the free and paid plans, and a failed payment will keep the previous plan unchanged. A downgrade will turn the paid features off but will keep all sales and inventory data, so that the tenant loses nothing if it upgrades again later. Alerts will notify users when new recommendations are raised or when an upload or forecasting job has finished.

\section{Test Metrics}

The test metrics in Tables~\ref{tab:metrics-functional} and~\ref{tab:metrics-nonfunctional} will be filled in after the test cases are executed in FYP-2, and only the planned number of test cases is given at this stage. \chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

The development effort spent in FYP-1 focused mainly on requirements, methodology and design aspects of StockSense, and consequently, none of the planned forecasting and recommendation experiments were conducted. In this chapter we describe each individual experiment, along with the data used in it, experimental setup, and the table in which results of the experiment will be entered. All the result fields are labelled ``To be filled'' at this point and will be filled in FYP-2 according to the iterative process described in Section~\ref{sec:iterative}. The decision to fixate the experimental design at this point brings two advantages. On one hand, the fixed experimental design ensures that the experiments will validate particular decisions taken during the model construction in Chapter~5, and not just the resulting models, and on the other hand, it will ensure that the evaluation is fixed before the results are known, and cannot therefore be changed afterwards to adjust to the results. The chapter begins with statement of research questions answered by the experiments, and continues with description of datasets and data preparation procedure, experimental design and evaluation protocol and descriptions of all five experiments.

\section{Objectives of the Experiments}

Each experiment answers one out of five questions that stem from the proposed approach to the problem of inventory control for small-scale retailers. The first question asks whether there is added benefit of using global LightGBM model over baseline statistical forecasting for product/size/store. The second question asks whether having one global model per tenant is more beneficial than having individual models for each series when sales history is limited. The third question asks for how long the sales history is sufficient for making a tenant new before we could have reliable forecast, so we'll know how long the client should wait after opening a business to receive predictions based on our models. The fourth question asks which loss function for LightGBM is the most appropriate for retail demand - Tweedie, Poisson or mean squared. The fifth question asks whether redistribution and markdown recommendations really decrease dead stocks and missed sales or doing nothing would yield more revenue. Questions one to four are related to the accuracy of forecast, whereas the fifth one is related to usefulness of the model.

\section{Experimental Setup}

Here we describe the dataset, pre-processing process, software and evaluation protocol which will be common for all the experiments.

\subsection{Datasets}

Three datasets will be used in the research. First, M5 dataset~\cite{makridakis2022m5}, publically available and containing daily sales, prices and calendar events of products in Walmart stores will be used for testing and training the models and it will provide store-level forecasting and higher hierarchies as well. It is not possible to evaluate behaviour of sizes here, so the dataset will be used just for initial training of the models. The other two sources of data are sales data from multi-branch retailer and small sample clients. If access to the first source of data is available, it is planned to use it and conclude only with respect to size-related questions. Small samples will be used to test the upload pipeline and recommendation logic end-to-end.

\subsection{Data Preparation}

Each series is then preprocessed and aggregated to weekly and monthly series according to the procedure outlined in Section~\ref{sec:series}. Smooth, erratic, intermittent and lumpy series are identified through the ADI and $\mathrm{CV}^2$ cutoff values of 1.32 and 0.49, respectively, in order to measure model accuracy independently for each demand class. Periods when stock-outs happen for a major part of time are regarded as censored and are omitted from the training process in order to exclude the fact that the observed sales underestimate true demand. All the features introduced in Section~\ref{sec:features} are then calculated for all series based on the historical data available at each forecasting moment. Experiments are conducted offline using Jupyter Notebook with Python, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, LightGBM and statsmodels. Random seeds are set in order to replicate results by each team member.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

The time splits are all chronological, so that the models are always trained on earlier horizons and validated on subsequent horizons. The hyperparameters of the models are tuned using a rolling origin validation procedure on an increasing time window, and in the final evaluation, the most recent horizon is used; this is never observed by any model during training or hyperparameter tuning. All models and baselines are always evaluated on exactly the same time splits, which ensures that any differences in performance can be attributed to the difference between the models and not to any difference in the data. Performance is measured in terms of the RMSE, MAE, WAPE, and non-zero MAPE defined in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, and the percentage of periods that were excluded due to zero demand in the actual forecast is also given when reporting the MAPE.

\section{Discussion}

After results have been obtained, the discussion will be concerned with three issues. First, it will be assessed whether LightGBM can be accurate enough relative to the baselines to compensate for the increased complexity of the model, which is especially important for intermittent and lumpy series, as simple models tend to perform well in such cases. Second, the discussion will consider how many historical periods a new tenant should have, because this will determine how long the onboarding process takes in UC-10 and thus how fast a new company recognizes the benefits from the paid functionalities. Third, whether the process of redistribution helps to lower the level of dead stock and missed sales compared to markdowns alone will be discussed, as this is the key proposition behind StockSense and its competitive advantage against the platforms considered in Chapter~3. The M5 results~\cite{makridakis2022m5} and the research on small businesses by Purnamasari et al.~\cite{purnamasari2023demand} make one think that gradient-boosted models will outperform statistical baselines, yet this belief has to be confirmed with our own experiments, especially in the case of short historical data.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

There are several possible threats to the generalizability of the results. First of all, the M5 dataset does not store information on product size, and thus any inference on demand by size can be made only on the basis of data from a real retailer. Sales in cases when a stockout has occurred underestimate demand, thus introducing bias in both forecasting and backtesting after exclusion of censored periods due to the fact that some short or partial stockouts might go unnoticed. Furthermore, the backtest is performed on the assumption of constant transfer lead time and constant price elasticity for markdowns, assumptions that do not necessarily hold in practice, and the obtained estimates of the effect should be treated only as indications and not exact forecasts. Results obtained on one particular retailer or dataset cannot be generalized to every single tenant, as businesses vary in terms of the product portfolio, seasonality, and number of branches.

\section{Conclusion}

The experiments used to assess StockSense were outlined in this chapter, which involved demand sparsity analysis, the comparison between LightGBM and statistical baselines, the comparison between global and separate models, the assessment of minimum training history, the comparison between loss functions, and a historical backtesting of the recommendations. Both datasets and data pre-processing, the evaluation process, and the result tables were predetermined to ensure the consistency of experiments carried out in FYP-2. Overall, the experiments will test all key decisions related to the methodology used in this research project, starting with the choice of the model and loss function used and ending with the priority given to redistribution. In addition to measuring the forecasting accuracy and its influence on dead stock and lost sales, the results will be included into the tables given above and discussed in FYP-2.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}

This chapter summarizes the work completed in FYP-1 and presents the plan for the work to be completed in FYP-2.

\section{Conclusion}

StockSense is proposed as a multi-tenant SaaS platform that helps small and medium-sized retailers manage their products, variants, inventory and stores in a single system. The platform combines a free POS and inventory layer with the upload of historical sales, demand forecasting at the product, size and location level, stock redistribution, markdown optimization, reorder recommendations and explanations for every recommended action. The proposed design addresses the main inventory problems of the target retailers. It uses sales data to reveal differences in demand between products, variants and branches, and it gives priority to moving available stock from a branch with a surplus to a branch with a shortage before suggesting markdowns or new orders. It also stores the reasons and estimated financial impact of each recommendation, so that business owners can understand and review the decisions of the system rather than follow a black box. The report has presented the project vision, the business opportunity, the related research and commercial products, the requirements, the methodology, the system architecture, the database design, the security controls, the testing strategy, the maintenance approach and the planned experiments.

The work completed in FYP-1 therefore covers the definition of the system scope, the review of competing platforms and previous research, the functional and non-functional requirements, the use cases, the data ingestion process, the forecasting and recommendation workflow, the evaluation protocol and the high-level and low-level design. The plan for FYP-2 follows the iterative approach described in Chapter~5. The backend, database, authentication, tenant isolation, dashboard, POS workflows and CSV and Excel upload pipeline will be implemented and integrated first, using a React dashboard, a Python REST backend, PostgreSQL, and pandas with openpyxl for data processing. The forecasting experiments of Chapter~9 will then be run, and the selected LightGBM model will be integrated together with its statistical baselines. After that, the redistribution recommendations, markdown optimization, reorder suggestions and explanation features will be developed. The completed system will be tested through unit, integration and end-to-end testing, evaluated with forecasting metrics and the historical backtest, and prepared for documentation, deployment and the final demonstration.

\section{Future Work}

Several extensions are planned beyond the scope of FYP-2. The first is to address the cold-start problem for businesses that have no past sales data at all, so that they can receive useful insights from the first day instead of waiting until enough history has been recorded. This could be done by using aggregated and anonymized patterns from similar businesses, with strict safeguards to preserve tenant isolation. The second is to make the platform compatible with a wide range of external POS systems through direct integrations, so that data can flow into StockSense automatically instead of being uploaded manually. The third is to add seasonal and promotional features that take into account changes in demand during holidays, festivals and different seasons, as well as the effect of discounts and special offers on sales. The fourth is to extend the redistribution model with transfer lead times and capacity limits, which are currently handled only as assumptions in the backtest. Together these extensions will help the system provide more accurate forecasts and more useful recommendations to a wider range of businesses.
